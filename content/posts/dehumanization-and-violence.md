In [The Architecture of Demoralization](https://saeidaliei.github.io/posts/architecture_of_demoralization/), 
it was explored how control can be exerted not through a locked door, 
but through the systematic erosion of a person’s social world and sense of self. 
The process begins with small acts of humiliation and escalates as the target’s support systems are severed. 
This essay extends that analysis to a broader scale, asking a related question: 
how does a society, or a group within it, arrive at a point where violence against others 
becomes not just possible, but permissible—even virtuous?

The answer lies in the process of **dehumanization**. It is the psychological architecture that constructs a road to violence. 
Dehumanization is not a single event but a continuum of thought and practice that denies others full humanity, 
placing them outside the moral community where empathy and restraint normally operate. 
This process is not confined to the extreme cases of genocide; it operates in everyday discrimination, 
in the rhetoric of political conflict, and in the quieter mechanisms of social exclusion that were the focus of the previous essay.

## The two faces of dehumanization

Social psychologist Nick Haslam’s influential model provides a crucial starting point by distinguishing two primary forms of dehumanization.

**Animalistic Dehumanization** likens others to animals. It denies them uniquely human traits such as rationality, 
civility, self-control, and moral sensibility. This form is often blatant and explicit, using metaphors of vermin, 
predators, or beasts. In the lead-up to the Rwandan genocide, Tutsis were routinely referred to as “cockroaches” (*inyenzi*), 
a dehumanizing label that prepared ordinary citizens to participate in their murder. 
This form of dehumanization is a staple of propaganda that seeks to overcome the normal human revulsion against killing 
by framing the victims as a threat or a pest to be exterminated.

**Mechanistic Dehumanization**, by contrast, is subtler. It denies traits of human nature such as warmth, 
emotion, and individuality, portraying others as cold, object-like, or machine-like. 
This form is less about overt hatred and more about indifference. It is the dehumanization of the bureaucrat 
who processes people as cases, or the institution that treats 
individuals as interchangeable units. While it may not directly incite violence, it erodes the capacity for empathy 
and moral concern, creating a background condition in which harm can be more easily ignored or justified.

## The psychological machinery: Moral disengagement

The link between dehumanizing perception and violent action is bridged by what Albert Bandura termed moral disengagement. 
This is not a sudden loss of morality, but a gradual process of cognitive restructuring that allows individuals 
to commit harmful acts without self-censure. Bandura identified several mechanisms, with dehumanization being one of the most powerful.

When a person or group is dehumanized, they are placed outside the scope of moral consideration. 
This makes it possible to harm them without experiencing the guilt or self-condemnation that would normally arise. 
This process can operate at three stages: reconstrue the conduct (e.g., “I am just following orders”), 
distort the consequences (e.g., “no one is really getting hurt”), and, crucially, devalue the victim (e.g., “they are not really human”).

Herbert Kelman’s classic work on “sanctioned massacres” provides a powerful sociological lens for this process. 
He argued that dehumanization deprives both victim and victimizer of identity and community, 
creating a psychological and social distance that makes violence possible. The victim is stripped of their individual identity, 
becoming an abstraction (“the enemy”), while the perpetrator is embedded in an institutional context that authorizes and normalizes the violence. 
This resonates deeply with the themes of coercive control: just as an abuser isolates their victim to control the narrative, 
a violent regime isolates its targets from the moral community to control the justification for harm.

## The road to violence: A continuum, not a cliff

The path from dehumanizing thought to violent action is not a sudden leap but a gradual ascent. 
The Pyramid of Hate model illustrates this escalation vividly. It begins with biased attitudes at the base—stereotypes and prejudiced 
beliefs that are often dismissed as “no big deal”. These attitudes then manifest as acts of bias, including name-calling, 
slurs, and social avoidance, where dehumanizing language is often first deployed.

The next level is discrimination, where bias is enacted in institutional and systemic ways, 
denying groups access to resources, opportunities, and rights. From there, 
the pyramid culminates in bias-motivated violence and finally genocide. 
Each level builds on the one below it, and the dehumanizing rhetoric that 
begins as a joke or a slur normalizes the more severe harms that follow.

This model aligns with the gradual escalation seen in interpersonal abuse. 
As it was argued in “The Architecture of Demoralization,” control begins with small boundary 
violations that become normalized. Similarly, on a societal scale, the road to violence 
is paved with small, often unnoticed, acts of dehumanization that steadily erode 
the moral restraints against harming others.

### A critical nuance: Dehumanization as cause and consequence

A sophisticated account must acknowledge a debate within the literature. 
While the standard model positions dehumanization as a preparatory step for violence, 
some scholars argue that in contexts like the Rwandan genocide, 
dehumanization was often a consequence of violence, not its cause. 
Perpetrators engaged in violence first, and dehumanizing language emerged afterward 
to justify and make sense of their actions.

This is not a contradiction but a complexity. The most accurate view is likely that dehumanization is multifunctional. 
It can act as a prelude, preparing the psychological ground for violence. 
It can facilitate violence in the present, as Haslam notes, by enabling perpetrators to 
overcome moral revulsion. And it can justify and minimize violence after the fact, 
allowing individuals and societies to reconcile their actions with their self-image. 
The road to violence is not a one-way street; dehumanization and violence can reinforce each other in a vicious cycle.

## Conclusion

The process of dehumanization is the architecture of moral exclusion. It constructs a world in which the suffering 
of others does not register as suffering, and in which violence becomes a permissible, 
even necessary, tool. It operates through the dual mechanisms of animalistic and mechanistic perception, 
is enacted through the psychological machinery of moral disengagement, and follows a gradual continuum from biased attitudes to genocide.

Understanding this process is not merely an academic exercise. It is a form of vigilance. 
The road to violence is not traveled only by monsters; it is built by ordinary people through ordinary psychological processes. 
Recognizing the early signs of dehumanization—in our language, our media, our political rhetoric, and our institutions—is the first 
step in refusing to travel that road. The opposite of dehumanization is not simply humanization; 
it is the active, daily practice of seeing others as fully human, with all the complexity, emotion, and moral worth that entails.
