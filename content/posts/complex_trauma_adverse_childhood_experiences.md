  When Survival Becomes a Way of Life:
  Childhood Adversity, Complex Trauma, and The Long Work of Recovery

A child rarely has the freedom to decide how to adapt to the world around them.
If care is unpredictable, attention may turn toward reading other people's
moods. If anger is dangerous, disagreement may become something to avoid. If
affection is conditional, being useful, quiet, successful, or agreeable can
begin to feel like the price of belonging.

These adaptations can make sense in the environment where they develop. The
difficulty comes when the environment changes but the adaptation remains. A
person may leave a threatening home and still expect danger in ordinary
disagreement. They may long for closeness while monitoring every sign of
rejection. They may feel guilty when resting, uncertain when treated kindly, or
ashamed of needs that were once ignored or punished.

The past does not need to be happening in the present to continue shaping how
the present is experienced.

## When adversity becomes a developmental environment

The term *adverse childhood experiences*, usually shortened to ACEs, refers to
potentially traumatic experiences during childhood. The original ACE study
examined categories including abuse and household dysfunction and found a graded
association between the number of adversity categories reported and the
prevalence of several adult health risks.[^1] Later public-health work has
connected childhood adversity with elevated risks across physical health, mental
health, relationships, and life opportunities, while also emphasizing prevention
and protective environments.[^2]

A single score, however, cannot describe a childhood. It does not fully show how
often something happened, how old the child was, whether a trusted adult was
available, how the child understood the experience, or whether safety followed.
Nor does it measure every source of adversity. Two people reporting the same
number of ACE categories may have had very different experiences and may respond
to them differently.

The wider point is not that every difficult childhood produces a disorder. It is
that prolonged exposure to threat, neglect, instability, or the absence of
reliable care can shape the conditions in which emotional and social development
take place. A child learns about safety and closeness not only from what adults
say, but from what repeatedly happens when the child is frightened, angry,
excited, needy, or vulnerable.

When the surrounding environment is consistently unsafe, survival is not a
temporary task. It can become the organizing problem around which daily life is
built.

## Complex trauma and the lasting effects of threat

Post-traumatic stress disorder is often described through symptoms such as
re-experiencing, avoidance, and a continuing sense of threat. The World Health
Organization's ICD-11 also recognizes complex post-traumatic stress disorder
(C-PTSD). In addition to core PTSD symptoms, C-PTSD includes persistent
difficulties with emotional regulation, a negative sense of self, and
difficulties maintaining relationships.[^3]

C-PTSD is associated particularly with prolonged or repeated traumatic
experiences from which escape is difficult, including repeated interpersonal
violence or childhood abuse. The diagnosis does not mean that every person with
childhood adversity has C-PTSD. Nor is it simply a more dramatic name for
unhappiness, low self-esteem, or any difficult family history. It is a clinical
concept with defined features, and assessment matters. Diagnostic systems also
differ: C-PTSD is a distinct diagnosis in ICD-11, whereas the DSM-5 does not
list it as a separate diagnosis.[^4]

What makes the concept useful is its recognition that trauma may affect more
than a person's memories of particular events. It can influence how someone
regulates emotion, understands their own worth, anticipates other people, and
responds to closeness or conflict. A person can know intellectually that a
danger has passed and still experience ordinary uncertainty as threatening.
Another may feel detached or emotionally blank, as though the safest response is
to reduce feeling before it can become overwhelming.

These responses are not evidence of weakness. They can be understood as patterns
learned under pressure. But understanding their origins does not mean they are
harmless, inevitable, or impossible to change.

## The coping strategy that outlives its purpose

People rarely choose coping patterns from a position of calm neutrality. They
develop them under constraints, using the options that seem available at the
time. A child who cannot leave may appease. A child whose feelings are mocked
may stop showing them. Someone who is repeatedly disappointed may stop expecting
help. Another person may become vigilant, controlling, perfectionistic, funny,
self-sufficient, or unusually attentive to what everyone else needs.

From the outside, these patterns can look like personality. Sometimes they even
bring praise: being undemanding, never causing trouble, always taking
responsibility, or being able to work through exhaustion. But a pattern can be
rewarded by others and still carry a cost for the person using it.

Research reviews have found associations between childhood maltreatment and
difficulties with emotion regulation and coping, including greater reliance on
avoidance and emotional suppression.[^5] Those findings do not mean every
instance of avoidance or suppression is pathological. These strategies can be
useful in particular circumstances. The question is whether a person can choose
among responses or feels compelled to use the same one regardless of what the
current situation requires.

A strategy that once reduced danger can become a restriction. Appeasement may
prevent immediate conflict but make it difficult to recognize one's own
preferences. Emotional suppression may help someone get through a crisis but
make it harder to identify distress before it becomes unmanageable.
Hypervigilance can make a person observant while keeping them exhausted.
Avoidance may provide short-term relief while allowing fear to govern more and
more of life.

The problem is not that the person adapted. The problem is that the adaptation
may have become rigid, operating long after the original conditions have
changed.

## The self that develops around other people's responses

Childhood also teaches a person what to expect from relationships. When comfort
is generally available, distress can become something that is shared and
managed. When care is unpredictable, a child may learn to intensify signals,
conceal needs, avoid dependence, or alternate between seeking closeness and
withdrawing from it. These are not simple, one-to-one outcomes; temperament,
later relationships, culture, and material circumstances all influence the
picture.

Repeated criticism, humiliation, or conditional approval can also influence the
way a person evaluates the self. Instead of thinking, "I did something wrong,"
they may come to feel, "There is something wrong with me." Shame is especially
powerful when it shifts from an evaluation of an action to a global conclusion
about a person's worth. A person may then hide mistakes, seek constant approval,
tolerate poor treatment, or avoid situations in which they might be seen
clearly.

In C-PTSD, negative self-concept and persistent difficulties in relationships
are not incidental details; they are part of the disturbances in
self-organization described in the ICD-11 framework.[^3] Clinical guidance
likewise identifies emotional dysregulation, dissociation, interpersonal
difficulty, and negative self-perception among the problems that may accompany
PTSD and complex presentations.[^4]

This helps explain why recovery can feel more complicated than recognizing that
the past was harmful. The person may have to reconsider assumptions that once
felt like facts: that a need is burdensome, that disagreement leads to
abandonment, that rest is laziness, that being useful is the only way to be
loved, or that closeness always comes with a hidden cost.

Those beliefs may feel deeply personal. Yet they can also be learned
expectations, formed through repetition rather than freely chosen after
reflection.

## Family history, adversity, and personality disorders

Mental-health difficulties do not usually emerge from a single cause. They can
develop through interactions among inherited vulnerabilities, temperament,
development, relationships, stressful experiences, and the conditions in which
a person grows up. Family history can matter, but it is not a script that a
child is destined to follow. Genetic influence and childhood environment are
not competing explanations: both can contribute, and they can interact.

Borderline personality disorder (BPD) is a useful example. The National
Institute of Mental Health identifies having a close family member, such as a
parent or sibling, with BPD as one factor associated with increased risk. A
large Swedish register study also found that BPD diagnoses clustered in
families, with estimates of familial risk declining as genetic relatedness
decreased.[^13][^14] These findings support the idea of familial vulnerability;
they do not mean that a child will necessarily develop BPD if a parent has it.

The term *heritability* is often misunderstood here. It describes how much of
the variation in a trait or disorder, within a particular population and set of
conditions, is statistically associated with genetic differences. It does not
tell us what proportion of one person's condition was caused by genes, and it
is not the probability that a particular child will inherit a diagnosis. BPD is
not explained by a single gene. Research points instead to multiple genetic
influences, alongside environmental contributions and partial overlap with
genetic risks for other traits and disorders.[^14][^15]

A parent's diagnosis also cannot, by itself, tell us what kind of parent they
are. People with BPD are not all alike, and a diagnosis is not proof of abuse,
neglect, or an inability to care for a child. The relevant family context may
include many things: the parent's symptoms and support, the stability of the
household, exposure to frightening or invalidating interactions, other sources
of stress, and whether the child has dependable adults and room to develop
independently. A child's risk may be influenced by inherited sensitivity, by
what happens in the environment, or by the interaction between them. These
possibilities need to be considered without turning a statistical association
into a judgment about an individual family.

### Can childhood adversity contribute to other personality disorders?

Research associates childhood maltreatment and neglect with later personality
pathology, but the strength and specificity of the evidence vary. The
association has been studied most extensively for BPD. Studies have also
reported links between different forms of maltreatment and antisocial,
avoidant, dependent, schizotypal, and other personality-disorder symptoms or
diagnoses. These findings do not establish a one-to-one pathway from a
particular adverse experience to a particular disorder.[^16][^18]

The quality of the evidence matters. An umbrella review of meta-analyses found
that, although many environmental factors had been studied, the strongest
support was concentrated in a limited number of associations involving BPD;
many other associations had weaker evidence, and the review noted that much
research measured personality traits rather than diagnosed disorders.[^16] A
2025 systematic review likewise found that the studies it included on the
combined relationship among childhood adversity, emotion dysregulation, and
personality disorder focused on BPD and were cross-sectional. Its additional
analysis suggested that emotion dysregulation may help explain links between
childhood abuse and a wider range of personality-disorder traits, but that
result did not carry over in the same way when adversity was measured more
broadly.[^17] The field is developing, and claims about disorders beyond BPD
should reflect those limits.

Adverse childhood experiences are better understood as risks that can affect
several areas of development, not as a diagnostic recipe. Depending on the
person and their circumstances, adversity may be associated with PTSD,
depression, anxiety, substance-use problems, relationship difficulties,
personality-disorder traits, a diagnosable disorder, or a combination of these.
Some people exposed to adversity develop none of these conditions. Others may
experience substantial difficulties without meeting criteria for a particular
diagnosis. Exposure alone cannot decide which outcome will occur.

### Comorbidity is not the same as inherited risk

*Comorbidity* means that more than one disorder or health condition occurs in
the same person. For example, BPD may co-occur with depression, anxiety,
post-traumatic stress, eating disorders, or substance-use disorders.[^13] This
is different from saying that a child is at increased risk because a parent
has BPD. The first describes conditions that coexist in one individual; the
second describes familial risk across generations.

Conditions can co-occur because they share some risk factors, because one
creates stress that worsens another, or because their symptoms overlap. The
presence of one diagnosis does not prove that it caused another, and symptoms
such as self-harm, emotional instability, dissociation, or substance use do
not by themselves establish a personality disorder. Careful assessment is
needed to understand the person's history, current symptoms, functioning, and
other possible explanations.

The point is not to deny familial patterns or the lasting effects of childhood
adversity. It is to avoid turning risk into fate. A family history can be a
reason to notice difficulties earlier and make support available, not a
prediction of who a child will become. Stable care, meaningful relationships,
appropriate treatment, and safer environments can all matter; the cycle is not
required to repeat itself.

## Why relief can become a trap

When distress persists, people naturally look for ways to change how they feel.
Some strategies offer quick relief: withdrawing, overworking, seeking
reassurance, using alcohol or other substances, engaging in compulsive
behaviours, or pursuing intense relationships that temporarily make emptiness
or uncertainty easier to bear. These behaviours do not all have the same cause,
and addiction should not be reduced to a single trauma explanation. But for
some people, a behaviour becomes powerful partly because of the state it
produces or the feeling it helps them escape.

This is one point at which trauma and substance use can intersect. A substance
may temporarily quiet intrusive memories, anxiety, shame, loneliness, or an
unbearable sense of inner agitation. Relief can be real, even when it is brief.
With repeated use, however, the costs may accumulate: relationships can suffer,
health and daily functioning may deteriorate, and the consequences of use may
create more distress that makes the next escape feel necessary. This pattern is
not the explanation for every addiction, and substance use can have many
interacting biological, psychological, and social causes.[^9][^10]

A similar loop can appear in behaviours that are not substance-related. A
person might withdraw just when connection begins to feel possible, abandon a
goal when success becomes attainable, or seek reassurance in ways that strain a
relationship. The term *self-sabotage* is often used for such patterns, but it
is a broad everyday label rather than a diagnosis. It can conceal very
different processes: fear of failure, fear of visibility, expectations of
rejection, difficulty tolerating uncertainty, practical obstacles, or an
attempt to regain control before someone else can cause hurt.

From the outside, these actions may appear irrational or deliberately
self-defeating. From the inside, they may offer some immediate protection:
leaving first can seem safer than being left; not trying can avoid the risk of
failure; rejecting help can avoid the vulnerability of depending on someone.
That does not mean every harmful choice is caused by trauma or that a person's
intentions can be inferred from their behaviour alone. The useful question is
what happens just before the pattern, what relief it provides in the short term,
and what it costs over time.

The cycle can become especially painful when a person then condemns themselves
for needing the very behaviour they rely on to cope. Distress prompts an action;
the action brings temporary relief; its consequences create more distress; and
self-criticism adds another burden. A trauma-informed approach asks what
function a behaviour serves as well as what harm it causes. That question does
not excuse harm or remove responsibility. It can make change more possible by
identifying the feelings, needs, and conditions that must be addressed if the
behaviour is to become less necessary.

## When distress turns against the self

For some people, the consequences of adversity are not limited to anxiety,
avoidance, or difficulty trusting other people. Distress can also become a way
of relating to the self. A person may treat mistakes as proof of worthlessness,
feel disgust toward their own needs, or assume that suffering is deserved.
What began as criticism or rejection from others can become an internal voice
that continues the work even when those people are absent.

### Self-hatred and internalized shame

There is a difference between regretting an action and treating the whole self
as irredeemably bad. Shame can move from “I did something wrong” to “I am
wrong.” When this becomes a stable self-concept, kindness may feel undeserved,
compliments may be dismissed, and ordinary mistakes may trigger disproportionate
self-punishment. In complex PTSD, a persistently negative view of oneself is one
of the disturbances in self-organization described by the ICD-11 framework.[^3]

Self-hatred is not an inevitable consequence of childhood adversity, and it is
not proof of a particular diagnosis. It may also be shaped by depression,
ongoing abuse, discrimination, loss, or other experiences. But when someone has
repeatedly been blamed, humiliated, or made responsible for other people's
behaviour, self-condemnation can become familiar. It can even feel safer to
believe “the problem is me” than to face the uncertainty of recognizing that
caregivers or other important people were unreliable or harmful.

### Self-harm and the attempt to regulate unbearable states

Self-harm also needs to be understood without either romanticizing it or
reducing it to attention-seeking. People harm themselves for different reasons.
For some, it may briefly interrupt emotional numbness, reduce overwhelming
arousal, express pain that feels impossible to put into words, or enact
self-punishment. The function can vary from person to person and from one
episode to another; it should not be assumed from the outside.[^11][^12]

Self-harm and suicidal intent are not interchangeable concepts. An episode of
self-harm does not, by itself, establish that a person intended to die. At the
same time, self-harm can cause serious injury, and a history of it is associated
with increased suicide risk. It is an important signal that the person deserves
careful, non-judgmental assessment and support. The relevant questions are not
merely “Why would someone do this?” but “What was happening beforehand?”, “What
did the behaviour change in that moment?”, and “What safer forms of support
could address the same need?”[^11][^12]

### Suicidal thoughts, hopelessness, and entrapment

When distress becomes prolonged, a person may begin to see the future as closed
and the present as impossible to bear. Suicidal thoughts can occur in the
context of traumatic experiences, depression, substance use, isolation,
hopelessness, or several of these at once. Adverse childhood experiences and
substance use are among the risk factors identified by public-health guidance,
but risk factors are not destiny and cannot determine an individual's future on
their own.[^9][^10]

It is important not to describe suicidal ideation simply as a wish for death
or to assume that it follows inevitably from trauma. For some people, such
thoughts express a wish for pain, fear, or entrapment to stop; for others, the
experience may be different. The meaning cannot safely be inferred from a
person's history alone. Suicidal thoughts deserve direct, compassionate
attention in their own right, rather than being treated only as another symbol
of past adversity.

The same principle applies to recovery more broadly: explanation is not a
substitute for care. Self-hatred, substance misuse, self-harm, and suicidal
ideation are not moral failures, nor are they problems that insight alone will
necessarily resolve. They warrant support proportionate to the person's needs,
including professional assessment when safety is at stake. Understanding how a
pattern developed can be part of recovery, but the immediate task is also to
help the person remain safe and find alternatives that work in the present.

## Needs are not indulgences

Safety, affection, rest, acceptance, autonomy, belonging, play, purpose, and the
ability to experience pleasure are not luxuries added after survival is
complete. They are part of ordinary human life. Yet in a threatening or
neglectful environment, some needs may be ignored, treated as selfish, or met
only when the child behaves in an approved way. A person may then become skilled
at noticing what others need while remaining uncertain about their own needs.

This way of thinking has become more visible in popular trauma education. Tim
Fletcher, whose work focuses on complex trauma and addiction recovery, uses a
framework of twelve basic needs—including safety, rest, acceptance, belonging,
purpose, pleasure, and connection—to help people examine how early experiences
may shape later coping and relationships.[^6] His framework is one accessible
teaching map among many; the specific twelve-part structure is his educational
model, not a standard diagnostic system. Its central question is broader than
the list itself: what did a person learn to do when a need could not be
expressed or reliably met?

The value of naming needs is practical. It can turn a vague sense that something
is missing into more precise questions. Do I feel safe enough to rest? Can I say
no without anticipating catastrophe? Do I have relationships in which care is
reciprocal? Can I enjoy something without feeling guilty? Can I recognize what I
want before adapting myself to what someone else wants?

There is no single list that defines every person's needs in exactly the same
way. The point is to restore curiosity about the parts of life that survival may
have pushed aside.

## Recovery is not just insight

Understanding the origin of a pattern can be a relief. It can replace a moral
verdict with a more accurate explanation: a response that looks irrational now
may once have helped someone manage an unsafe situation. But insight is only one
part of change. Knowing why a person freezes in conflict does not automatically
make it possible to respond differently when conflict occurs.

Recovery may involve learning to recognize emotional states, tolerate distress
without immediately escaping it, set boundaries, question entrenched beliefs,
and build relationships in which disagreement does not automatically threaten
belonging. It may also require practical changes: stable housing, medical care,
safer surroundings, financial security, or distance from ongoing abuse.
Psychological work cannot substitute for the material conditions that make
safety possible.

Trauma-informed care emphasizes safety, trust, collaboration, empowerment, and
reducing the risk of retraumatization.[^7] These principles have a direct
bearing on recovery: a process intended to help someone regain agency should not
rely on humiliation, forced disclosure, unquestionable authority, or the removal
of choice. People need room to make decisions about their own lives, including
the pace and kind of help they receive.

There are established psychological treatments for PTSD, and clinical guidance
recommends assessing the individual's symptoms, needs, and circumstances rather
than applying one uniform pathway to everyone.[^4] Research on interventions
specifically for C-PTSD is continuing to develop. A 2025 systematic review found
a small number of studies that met its inclusion criteria, covering several
therapeutic approaches, while noting the need for more research on the
distinctive difficulties associated with C-PTSD.[^8] This does not mean there
are no useful treatments. It means that claims about a particular method should
be judged by the evidence for that method, and that care should be adapted to
the person rather than to a slogan or a single model.

Supportive relationships can matter as well. A safe relationship does not erase
the past, and no friend or partner should be expected to act as a therapist. But
dependable experiences of respect, boundaries, repair, and mutuality can provide
a different basis for learning what relationships can be. Change is often less
about discovering one hidden explanation than about having repeated
opportunities to practise a response that was previously unavailable.

## The past is not a life sentence

The effects of childhood adversity are real, but risk is not destiny. People
differ in the conditions they experienced, the support available to them, the
ways they made sense of events, and the opportunities they later encounter. Some
will recognize themselves in descriptions of complex trauma; others may find
that different explanations better account for their difficulties. No checklist
can settle that question on its own.

The more useful shift is from asking what is wrong with a person to asking how
their current patterns developed, what those patterns accomplish, and whether
they still serve them. That question preserves both compassion and agency. It
takes the past seriously without making it the only story available.

An adaptation can be understandable without being permanent. A response can have
protected someone in one environment and be costly in another. With appropriate
support, safety, and practice, people can develop new ways of regulating
distress, relating to others, and deciding what matters to them.

The aim is not to erase the past or turn every struggle into a trauma story. It
is to understand enough of the past to make more choices in the present.
