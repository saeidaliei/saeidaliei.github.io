title: "Mirror Neurons"
date: 2026-10-27
---

Watch someone reach for a cup. Your brain does not necessarily treat the
movement as a purely visual event. Some of the neural machinery involved in
performing an action also responds when you observe someone else performing it.
In the early 1990s, researchers discovered neurons in the premotor cortex of
macaque monkeys that responded both when the animal grasped an object and when
it watched another individual do the same. They called them *mirror neurons*.
[^1]

The discovery was real. The explanations built on it became considerably more
ambitious.

## Why would evolution produce them?

Imagine living in a small hunter-gatherer group. Your survival depends partly
on predicting what other people will do. You need to coordinate a hunt, learn
skills by watching others, recognize when someone is frightened, and anticipate
whether another person's actions are threatening or cooperative.

A brain that efficiently connects the observation of actions with its own
motor representations could be useful in this environment. More broadly,
natural selection had reasons to favour the ability to learn from others,
anticipate their behaviour, and navigate social relationships.

But there is a distinction between explaining why a capacity might be useful
and explaining how it evolved. Mirror neurons have been discovered in macaques,
so mirror-like mechanisms are not uniquely human. Their evolutionary history
may extend well before our species. Moreover, some researchers argue that
mirror properties can emerge through associative learning: repeatedly seeing
actions while performing them gradually connects their sensory and motor
representations. On this account, evolution may have provided the learning
machinery without designing a dedicated system for empathy. [^2]

The same evolutionary reasoning applies to tribalism. Recognizing who belongs
to your group, learning its conventions, and cooperating with its members
could all have been advantageous. But this does not mean that evolution
produced one specific mechanism for tribalism, just as it does not establish
that mirror neurons evolved specifically for empathy.

## Empathy as a defence mechanism

There is another possibility worth considering. What if sensitivity to other
people's behaviour is not always about cooperation or empathy, but sometimes
about survival?

Imagine a child growing up in an unpredictable environment. A parent's mood
can change suddenly. The tone of a voice, a facial expression, the sound of
footsteps, or a seemingly insignificant movement can signal whether something
pleasant or frightening is about to happen.

For such a child, accurately anticipating other people's behaviour becomes
unusually important. Missing a signal may have consequences. The child may
therefore become highly attentive to subtle changes in other people's
expressions, voices, and movements, constantly trying to predict what will
happen next.

This is not merely speculation about development. Research on childhood
adversity suggests that threatening environments can shape developing stress
and emotion-processing systems. Some studies have found altered responses to
emotional faces, including heightened sensitivity to potential threats. But
the findings do not imply that adversity reliably produces a general ability
to read other people more accurately. [^3]

The interesting distinction is between empathy and hypervigilance. Someone
who anticipates other people's emotional states may genuinely be empathetic,
but they may also be monitoring those states because doing so once helped them
feel safe. What looks like an exceptional ability to understand others can
sometimes be partly a learned strategy for predicting danger.

Could mirror neurons be involved in this process? Possibly, as part of the
broader machinery connecting the perception of actions with our own
representations of them. But there is no strong evidence that mirror neurons
specifically cause developmental hypervigilance. Attention, learning, memory,
and the brain's threat-processing systems are also likely to matter.

Perhaps, then, some of the same social sensitivity that enables cooperation
can also be recruited for self-protection. The capacity to notice another
person's intentions is useful in either case; what differs is the problem the
individual is trying to solve.

## What do we actually know?

The strongest evidence for mirror neurons concerns the relationship between
observing and performing actions. Claims that they explain empathy,
intention-reading, or even language require additional evidence. In humans,
many studies rely on fMRI and other indirect measures, which cannot ordinarily
establish that the same individual neurons respond during both action and
observation.

Research continues to refine the picture. A central question is whether
mirror-like responses are largely an evolved specialization, a product of
sensorimotor learning, or some combination of the two. These explanations
are not necessarily mutually exclusive: evolution can shape the learning
mechanisms through which experience builds neural representations. [^2]

The evidence therefore supports a more modest conclusion than the popular
story suggests. Mirror neurons may be one component of a much broader system
through which we learn from, predict, and respond to others. They are not, by
themselves, an explanation for human sociality.

Perhaps the deeper question is how evolution and experience interact to shape
the ways we understand one another. Some capacities may have been favoured
because they helped our ancestors cooperate. Others may emerge, or become
exaggerated, because an individual's environment makes certain skills
especially important. The same sensitivity can serve different purposes in
different contexts.

A mechanism that helps us anticipate another person's actions is not
necessarily a mechanism for empathy. And a tendency that was useful in one
environment may become costly in another.

Understanding human behaviour may therefore require more than asking what a
trait evolved for. We must also ask how it develops, what problems it solves,
and what happens when the environment changes.
