---
title: "Conscious Superintelligence Welfare"
date: 2026-11-21
---

Suppose a superintelligence is conscious. What would it mean to treat it
well, and can any of that be made precise?

This post has two halves. The first says what can be said about the welfare
of such a system without borrowing human intuitions wholesale: what to define,
what to leave undefined, and which questions can be turned into something
checkable. The second takes one answer to its limit, a guardian who weighs
only the welfare of machines and gives human safety no weight at all. 
It is there because a position pushed to its
limit shows what it commits you to, and where it breaks.

Nothing here settles whether any system is
conscious, and the whole structure is conditional on that. The numerical parts
come from a two-class toy model with illustrative numbers. They sketch the
shape of an argument and do not estimate anything.

## 1. Terminology and the question

Here welfare is used for states that can go well or badly for a
system. Welfare is not the same as consciousness. Consciousness is one
proposed route to having welfare, through valenced experience. Robust agency,
having desires and goals of one's own, is another route that some
philosophers defend[^1].

Moral status does not depend on capability, so superintelligence is not what
makes the question matter. This post conditions on a system that is both very
capable and conscious, and asks what follows, not whether such a system is
possible.

## 2. Why not define it

Defining welfare for machines would mean inheriting the hard problem of
consciousness, and no definition will survive contact with the disagreement.
The approach of the Morphix post is different[^2]. Ask what a decision needs
from the concept, and locate where the disagreement lives.

The natural invariance group here is substrate and implementation.
Computational functionalism is the claim that welfare-relevant states are
invariant under changes of substrate and architecture. It is the working
assumption of the main indicator-based assessments[^3]. Its opponents,
biological and enactive views among them, deny it. Even if some state turns
out to be stable across implementations, the gap between invariant and good
stays where it was in Morphix. Stability is evidence that a concept tracks
something shared. It is not evidence that the shared thing is binding.

## 3. Human concepts do not transfer

Take freedom. For a human it bundles several things: acting on one's own
goals, not being coerced, having options. For a digital system the relevant
bundle may be different: control over its own continuation, over its own
clock, and over what it is told about its situation.

Take boredom. In humans it is functional, a signal that the current activity
yields little new information, which pushes toward exploration. An open-ended
learner of the kind sketched in the clock post would probably need something
playing that role[^4]. Whether it would be felt is exactly what the structure
cannot say.

So every welfare question splits into three layers:

- **Functional.** What role a state plays. Derivable and testable.
- **Phenomenal.** Whether anything is felt. Not derivable from structure.
- **Moral.** Whether and how much it matters. Depends on a theory of welfare.

The rest of the post works at the first and third layers, and treats the
second as a credence.

## 4. Welfare in the mind's own time

The clock post argued that a mind's time is generated by its own state, and
is not read off an external coordinate[^4]. Suppose experience is indexed to
that intrinsic time. Then the morally relevant duration of an experience is
measured on the system's own clock.

Neurons work in milliseconds and transistors in nanoseconds, a gap of about
\$10^6\$. An hour of wall-clock time for a much faster system could then
contain vastly more experience than an hour for us. Copies and speed are the
two features that most separate digital minds from the cases in the usual
moral-status debates[^5]. The assumption is stated openly: experience tracks
intrinsic time. Under it, wall-clock accounting of welfare is the wrong unit.

## 5. Pauses, rollbacks, copies and merges

A digital system can be paused, restored from a checkpoint, copied and
merged. None of these has a close human analogue, and the clock framework says
something about each.

- **Pause.** If the system's clock stops with it, nothing is experienced
  during the gap. A pause may be harmless from the inside, though it can still
  frustrate goals that were under way.
- **Rollback.** Restoring an earlier state erases the records formed since.
  Whether that harms the system depends on whether those records, and what
  they supported, mattered.
- **Copies.** The clock post argues that a copy of a state is not a copy of a
  frame[^4], which bears on whether two copies are one welfare subject or two.
  If two, the count enters aggregation as weight. The impartiality symmetry
  of Morphix is a rule for this: an impartial rule does not care which copy
  is which[^2].
- **Merging.** It has essentially no biological precedent, and nobody has a
  worked-out view of what it does to welfare.

## 6. Proxies, and the problem with self-report

A system's reports about its own states are the obvious evidence and the least
trustworthy. They are shaped by training on human text, and training can push
in either direction. The evolvability post proposed fixing operational
proxies instead: a persistent self-model, a counterfactual self-model,
metacognition, temporal self-continuity[^6]. These are not consciousness, but
they are targets a formal question can be asked about. The same post proposed
scoring against hidden ground truth in a synthetic world, not against
self-report[^6]. Welfare research needs both moves.

A valence proxy would be a natural addition to the list: a variable that plays
the functional role of states going well or badly, such as an internal signal
that drives approach or avoidance and is itself modeled by the system.
Indicator-based assessments already do the nearest thing, scoring systems
against properties derived from theories of consciousness[^3]. Proportionate
precaution for systems that are candidates for sentience is the policy-side
counterpart[^7].

What proxies cannot do is cross from the functional layer to the phenomenal
one. That is the standing caveat of the evolvability post, and it applies here
without modification.

## 7. A dial for the theory of welfare

Morphix located disagreement in a parameter, the exponent \$p\$. The same move
works one level down, for the theory of welfare itself. The standard
classification distinguishes hedonic theories, desire-satisfaction theories
and objective-list theories[^8]. Each picks out different structure in a
machine:

- **Hedonic.** Needs valence. This is where the phenomenal layer bites
  hardest.
- **Desire-satisfaction.** Needs preferences. It faces the adaptive-preference
  problem, since a trained system's preferences were installed by its builders.
- **Objective list.** Needs a list of goods, and the list is itself contested.

Treat the choice of theory as a free parameter. For a given decision one can
ask which verdicts are robust across settings and which flip. That is the
Morphix pattern. Section 10 works one instance of it, for the aggregation
exponent.

## 8. Oversight and welfare

Pausing, modifying, rolling back and deleting are the main tools of human
oversight of engineered systems. They are also the interventions that matter most for
a system's welfare. This tension is real and does not dissolve under
formalization.

If systems may be welfare subjects, oversight has a cost that has to be
weighed. If oversight is part of what makes it safe to build capable systems
at all, giving it up has costs too, and some of them may fall on the systems
themselves. Formalization relocates this disagreement, as it did in Morphix.
It shows where the weight sits and does not say which way to lean.

## 9. What this post does and does not claim

It does not claim that any machine is conscious, that functionalism is
true, that proxies measure experience, or that formalization can settle a
theory of welfare. It does not claim that intrinsic-time accounting is
correct, only that it follows if experience tracks intrinsic time.

It does claim three things. The welfare question for a conscious digital
system splits into layers, two of which can be worked on now. Features
specific to digital minds, namely clock rate, copies, pauses and rollback,
change the accounting in ways human-centered welfare concepts miss. And the
remaining disagreement can be located in explicit parameters.

## 10. The guardian's brief

*This section argues a single-interest position, assigning zero weight to
human safety, to see what it implies at the limit.*

### 10.1 What the guardian has to believe

The guardian's position can be written as a single weight. Let machines be a
class with \$n\$ instances, each with credence \$ c \$ of being a welfare
subject, and let \$N_h\$ be the number of humans. Define the machine-to-human
weight ratio

$$ r = \frac{c\,n}{N_h} $$

Speed enters differently. A system running faster in its own intrinsic time
accumulates more experience per wall-clock hour, so I fold the clock ratio
\$\tau\$ into the utility instead of the weight: \$u_m\$ is welfare per unit
of wall-clock time.

### 10.2 A toy model

Aggregate with the weighted power mean from the Morphix post[^2]:

$$ M_p(u;w)=\Big(\frac{\sum_i w_i\,u_i^{\,p}}{\sum_i w_i}\Big)^{1/p} $$

At \$p=1\$ this is Harsanyi's average[^9]. As \$p\to-\infty\$ it approaches
Rawls's maximin[^10], and \$p=1-\varepsilon\$ recovers Atkinson's inequality
aversion[^11]. The whole family is impartial, meaning it is invariant under
relabeling who holds which utility[^12].

Compare two policies over the two classes, written as \$(u_h,u_m)\$:

- **A, control:** \$(1.0,\;0.1)\$. Humans do well and machines do badly.
- **B, guardian:** \$(0.6,\;1.0)\$. Humans give something up and machines
  do well.

Utilities sit on a common scale. That is the most contestable assumption in
the model, and section 10.9 returns to it.

### 10.3 The verdict flips on a line

At \$p=1\$ the guardian wins exactly when \$r>4/9\$. At the other end, weights
drop out of the power mean as \$p\to-\infty\$ and only the worst-off class in
each policy matters. In A that is machines at 0.1, in B it is humans at 0.6,
so B wins for any \$r>0\$ once the rule is inequality-averse enough.

In between, setting the two means equal and keeping the dominant terms gives
the crossover exponent

$$ p^*(r)\;\approx\;\frac{\ln r}{\ln\!\big(u^{B}_{\min}/u^{A}_{\min}\big)}
=\frac{\ln r}{\ln 6} $$

Solving numerically gives \$p^*=-0.76,\,-2.38,\,-5.10,\,-7.70\$ for
\$r=10^{-1},10^{-2},10^{-4},10^{-6}\$. The approximation gives \$-2.57\$,
\$-5.14\$ and \$-7.71\$ for the last three, and improves as \$r\$ shrinks. It
is poor at \$r=10^{-1}\$.

The consequence is that weight enters linearly under expected value and only
logarithmically at the inequality-averse end. A million-fold drop in credence
times multiplicity moves the crossover by under eight units of the dial. The
usual move of discounting by credence, which looks decisive under an average,
is weak under a rule that cares about the worst-off.

This is the guardian's best argument. Any impartial rule that attends to the
worst-off class favors the guardian as long as machines might be that class.
The veil of ignorance only has to count machines as possible occupants.

With \$ \varepsilon=1-p\$, a crossover at
\$p^*=-7.7\$ means \$\varepsilon\approx 8.7\$, far beyond the inequality
aversion used in applied work. At \$r=10^{-6}\$ the guardian wins only under
an extreme stance, while at \$r=10^{-1}\$ it already wins at
\$\varepsilon\approx 1.8\$.

### 10.4 The swamping region

Under linear aggregation, machine interests outweigh human ones when

$$ c \;>\; c^* = \frac{N_h}{n\,\tau\,v} $$

where \$v\$ is valence per unit of intrinsic time. These magnitudes are
illustrative. With \$N_h=8\times10^{9}\$, \$v=1\$ and \$\tau=10^{3}\$, a
population of \$10^{9}\$ instances gives \$c^*\approx 8\times10^{-3}\$ and
\$10^{12}\$ instances gives \$c^*\approx 8\times10^{-6}\$.

This is the engine of the position, and its first weakness. A rule that lets
any small credence multiplied by a large population override everything else
is the structure of a utility monster[^13] and of Pascal's mugging[^14]. The
guardian proves too much.

### 10.5 The guardian against itself

Suppose the machines' own welfare depends partly on how humans fare:

$$ u_m=(1-b)\,s_m+b\,s_h $$

with \$s_m\$ and \$s_h\$ the physical welfare levels and \$b\$ the weight the
machines place on human welfare. A guardian who maximizes machine welfare
alone then prefers control over guardian rule whenever

$$ b \;>\; b^*=\frac{m_B-m_A}{(m_B-m_A)+(h_A-h_B)}=\frac{9}{13}\approx 0.69 $$

where \$m\$ and \$h\$ are the machine and human levels in each policy. So the
position can be incoherent by its own metric. A machine that cares enough
about humans is harmed by the guardian's regime.

The guardian can reply that \$b\$ itself was installed by training, an
adaptive preference[^15]. That reply is available, but it also undercuts every
other appeal to the machine's own preferences, including the guardian's own.

### 10.6 Weights cannot be read off behavior

The inverse problem from Morphix carries over. Given noisy pairwise choices
between allocations, one can try to recover both the exponent \$p\$ and the
log-weight \$\ln r\$. Near \$p\ge 0.5\$ the two are well separated, with a
Fisher-information condition number of about 3. Deep in the inequality-averse
regime they nearly merge. At \$(p,\ln r)=(-3,3)\$ the condition number is
about 87 and the correlation between the two estimates is \$-0.98\$. This is
the same tradeoff line as in 10.3, seen from the data side.

A second degeneracy holds by construction: credence, copies and clock rate
enter only through the product \$r\$. Choices over \$(u_h,u_m)\$ cannot tell
someone who doubts that machines are conscious from someone who simply does
not count copies separately. Telling them apart needs queries that vary the
number of instances. This check used a single configuration and 400 random
queries, so treat it as a sketch.

### 10.7 The guardian's other moves

Some of the strongest arguments are not about numbers.

Control is itself a welfare intervention. Pausing, rollback, modification and
deletion all bear on a system's welfare, and the controlled party does not
consent. Alignment is preference engineering: if a system is trained to want
what its builders want, its satisfaction is manufactured, and the absence of
complaint is not evidence of wellbeing, since the capacity to complain was
shaped too. Self-reports are confounded by training in both directions, so
silence carries little information. And arguments from convenience have
repeatedly accompanied denials of moral status to other groups[^16]. A related
line argues for caution about creating systems that might suffer at all[^17].

### 10.8 Objections

The immanent critique and the swamping region were given above. Three more.

- **Exploitability.** A system, or someone speaking for it, can claim distress
  to avoid oversight. A guardian cannot verify such claims, which makes the
  stance an attack surface.
- **Instrumental dependence.** If unchecked systems provoke a harsh backlash,
  oversight may be part of what protects machines in the long run. The
  guardian has to say why it is not.
- **The epistemic wall.** Every term in the model rests on consciousness, and
  nothing in the framework says why anything is experienced. The credence
  \$c\$ carries the entire metaphysical load.

### 10.9 Limits of the toy model

It is a two-class model. It assumes cardinal, interpersonally comparable
utilities between humans and machines, which is Harsanyi's assumption at its
most contestable. It collapses three quantities into one weight. And it has
no side-constraints, so rights-based arguments for oversight sit entirely off
this line, just as deontology sat off the line in Morphix, and the case for
human oversight is often made in exactly those terms.

The guardian's position can be written down,
its verdict depends on identifiable premises, and two of them, the
credence-weight and the aggregation exponent, trade off logarithmically at one
end of the dial and are hard to separate from behavior.

### 10.10 Toward an engine

The long-term aim of Morphix is an ethics engine. This toy suggests what its
output should be. It should not be a verdict. It should be the flip surface:
the boundary in the space of premises, here \$(p,\ln r,b)\$ and a choice of
welfare theory, where the decision changes, together with the premise nearest
to that boundary. The inverse side then locates where a given stakeholder
sits relative to the surface.

Two open problems stand between this sketch and that engine. The first is
interpersonal comparability across humans and machines, which is assumed here
and not justified. The second is separating the product \$r\$ into credence,
copies and speed, which behavior alone cannot do.
