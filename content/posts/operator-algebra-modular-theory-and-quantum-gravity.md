In quantum field theory, even defining the entropy of a region of space is more subtle than it first appears. 
The familiar quantum-mechanical picture, in which a system is described by a density matrix and its entanglement entropy calculated from it, 
does not carry over straightforwardly to local algebras in quantum field theory. 
These are typically type III von Neumann algebras, whose mathematical structure rules out that familiar description.

This is not merely a technical inconvenience. It becomes especially interesting when gravity enters the picture.

Recent work, notably the construction developed by Chandrasekaran, Longo, Penington, and Witten, 
shows how incorporating gravity and an observer can lead to a different algebraic structure: 
a type II algebra in which an appropriate notion of entropy becomes well-defined, this is not a mere decoration. 
Understanding this result requires a considerable amount of mathematics, most of which is unfamiliar to physicists 
trained primarily in quantum mechanics and quantum field theory.

That gap is what this book attempts to bridge.

The opening chapters build the mathematical foundations, beginning with Hilbert spaces, operator topologies, 
and the spectral theorem, then moving through \$C^*\$-algebras, states, representations, and von Neumann algebras. 
The classification into types I, II, and III is particularly important: 
it provides the vocabulary needed to understand why the usual treatment of entropy encounters difficulties.

The next part develops modular theory and crossed products. 
Tomita–Takesaki theory connects states to a canonical modular evolution, 
while crossed products provide the machinery for constructing new algebras 
from existing ones and their dynamics. These ideas lead naturally into algebraic 
quantum field theory, where locality, the structure of observables, 
and the type III nature of local algebras become central.

With this groundwork in place, the book turns to gravity and de Sitter space. 
The central construction is developed through gravitational constraints, edge modes, 
the role of an observer, and the resulting algebraic description. 
The discussion then examines generalized entropy, 
the interpretation of empty de Sitter space as a maximal-entropy state in the relevant type II\$_1\$ construction, 
and the questions that remain open.

The final chapters broaden the picture to blackholes and holography, 
including entanglement wedges and Hawking radiation, 
before introducing noncommutative geometry through spectral triples, 
the Dixmier trace, and the spectral action. 
A final chapter collects open problems and directions for further study.

The appendices provide mathematical background, worked solutions to the exercises, 
a glossary connecting mathematical and physical terminology, and an annotated bibliography. 
The book is assumes a graduate-level background in quantum mechanics and quantum field theory, 
but no prior training in functional analysis or topology.

The manuscript was developed with Claude Sonnet. 
It is a complete draft, not an independently peer-reviewed textbook; the source, revision history, 
and downloadable PDF builds are available in the [GitHub repository](https://github.com/saeidaliei/opalgebra-book).

The broader motivation is to make a specific chain of ideas accessible: 
how the mathematical structure of quantum observables constrains the quantities we can define, 
and how including gravity can change that structure. The mathematics is substantial, but the question driving it is physical.

Another important motivation includes our work in progress on modelling cognition, 
which the ideas developed in the book seems to become important.
