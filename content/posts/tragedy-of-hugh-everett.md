---
title: "The Tragedy That is Hugh Everett"
date: 2026-11-18
---
Hugh Everett believed that nothing is ever truly lost—that every possibility plays out in some universe or another. 
He spent the rest of his life proving, with his life, that this is no comfort at all in the one world you actually have to live in.

He solved the measurement problem by multiplying the universe. 
Where Bohr and the Copenhagen school had drawn a line between the quantum world and the classical observer, 
Everett erased it. The wavefunction never collapses, he said. 
Every possible outcome of every quantum event actually occurs, 
branching reality into a vast tree of mutually inaccessible worlds. 
The observer is not outside the system; 
the observer is part of the wavefunction, 
and every version of the observer sees a consistent history in their own branch.

But his tragedy has a distinct and uniquely cruel shape. 
He did not fail to find meaning in the world. 
He found a way to multiply meaning until it dissolved. 
In imagining everything that could happen, 
he lost his grip on the one world where he actually had to live—and the man who had solved 
the riddle of the universe could not solve the riddle of his own life.

## He who solved the measurement problem

In 1957, Everett submitted a PhD thesis at Princeton under Wheeler. 
It was a radical, almost insolent solution to the measurement problem. 
Wheeler tried to shield his student from the worst of the reaction, 
but the verdict from the Copenhagen "establishment" was dismissive 
The theory was called "stupid" by some of Bohr's circle. 
(I don't know why at that time they thought they are the gate-keeper of reality!). 
Everett was stung and left academia not long after, 
and went to work in nuclear strategy and computer science 
and never published another significant paper in physics.

Everett did not also ignore the question of consciousness. 
In the introductory part of his work, he discusses the "amusing, but extremely hypothetical drama" 
of the Wigner's friend paradox—a thought experiment in which a conscious observer is placed inside a quantum system, 
forcing the question of what happens when observation itself is observed. 
It was Everett who provided the first written discussion of this problem, 
years before Wigner published his famous "Remarks on the mind-body question." 
Everett's answer was not that consciousness collapses the wavefunction, 
but that consciousness is simply what it feels like to be a branch. 
Every version of the observer, in every branch, experiences a consistent world. 
The mind does not collapse reality. The mind is just one more thing that happens inside it.

## His tragedy

The man who had imagined an infinity of worlds retreated, in his own life, 
into a world of cold war calculations. Biographies of Everett paint a portrait of a man who was, 
in his own home, a kind of absent presence. He was physically there, but emotionally elsewhere. 
His son, Mark, now the frontman of the band Eels, described him as "a lump of furniture sitting at the dining room table."

Privately, Everett struggled deeply. 
He suffered from depression and became dependent on alcohol and cigarette, 
habits that would eventually contribute to his death. 
He was, by many accounts, a man who had solved the riddle of the universe 
and could not solve the riddle of his own life. He died of a heart attack at home in 1982, at fifty-one. 
He had requested that his ashes be thrown in the trash, a final gesture of atheistic, utilitarian pragmatism, 
and maybe utter meaninglessness he found himself in. 
But the tragedy of the Everett family did not end with his death. It deepened.

## The utmost tragedy

His daughter, Elizabeth—known as Liz—had struggled for years with schizophrenia and addiction. 
In 1996, fourteen years after her father died, she took her own life with an overdose of sleeping pills. 
She left a suicide note. In it, she wrote that she hoped to "end up in the correct parallel universe to meet up with Daddy." 
She asked that her ashes be disposed of in the same way as her father's—thrown out with the garbage—perhaps so that the Many-Worlds could carry her to him.

This is the point where the story becomes almost unbearable to hold. 
The Many-Worlds Interpretation is not merely a neutral mathematical formalism. 
It makes a claim about the structure of reality: that every possibility is realized somewhere. 
For a daughter who had lost her father—who had, by all accounts, never really had him—the theory offered a kind of consolation. 
Somewhere, in some branch, he was still alive. Somewhere, in some branch, he had been a father. 
Her death, in that reading, was not an ending but a transition, 
a way of navigating the tree to find the man who had been physically 
present but emotionally absent in every world she had ever inhabited.
Many-Worlds gave her a language for her longing, and it gave her a destination for her despair. 

## The unbearable infinity of branching

The tragedy of Hugh Everett is not that he was wrong or right. He may very well be right. 
The tragedy is that the establishment was so much proud that they would wrather call a perfectly well-suited theory "stupid". 
The establishment today in many ways has also continued that line.

He saw infinity and missed the one branch that had been entrusted to him. 
In the end, the Many-Worlds Interpretation is not a consolation. 
It is a mirror. It shows us that every choice we do not make is made somewhere else, 
and every version of ourselves that we fail to become exists in some inaccessible branch. 
For most of us, that is a philosophical curiosity. For the Everetts, 
it was a family tragedy—a tragedy written in the language of quantum and suffering, 
sealed with a suicide note that asked the universe to do what a father could not.

May his and her daughter's suffering be the meaning that they could not find.
