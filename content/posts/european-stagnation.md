title: "When a Civilization Mistakes Equilibrium for Progress: The Great European Stagnation"

A plastic cap is an oddly small object to carry a theory of Europe. Under an EU
rule, caps on certain single-use plastic beverage containers must remain
attached during intended use[^1]. The environmental rationale is intelligible;
so is the everyday irritation. The rule became a meme because it seems to stand
for something beyond the cap: a continent that appears increasingly skilled at
specifying how existing things should behave, and less convincing at producing
the next things the world will depend on.

Of course, a cap says almost nothing on its own. Neither does a slow train, a
planning delay, or the fact that many visible technology companies emerged
elsewhere. But when these impressions accumulate, they raise a question that
debates about taxes, regulation, and productivity do not fully capture. Can a
society become so invested in an equilibrium that it loses the ability to see
that equilibrium as provisional?

That is the thought I want to examine. Europe has not stopped developing, and
there are good reasons to value many of its choices. My concern is more
specific: it may be getting harder for its institutions and public imagination
to distinguish preserving a successful arrangement from remaining capable of
success under different conditions.

## A choice is not the same as a constraint

The familiar diagnosis is that Europe has chosen a comfortable life over a
dynamic one. There is something to this, but it risks confusing the visible
preference with the deeper mechanism. Leisure, social insurance, consumer
protection, environmental standards, and predictable lives are not inherently
signs of decadence. They are among the achievements that make a society worth
building. A society is not obliged to maximize GDP, work hours, or disruption.

The issue begins when preserving those achievements depends on capabilities a
society is no longer developing. Leisure is a dividend of productivity, capital,
and institutional competence; it cannot indefinitely substitute for them. A
social model can be a legitimate objective while still needing to change its
means as the environment changes.

There is a difference between saying, “We understand the trade-off and choose
this arrangement,” and saying, “Because this arrangement reflects our values,
evidence that it is becoming unsustainable does not really challenge it.” The
first is a choice. The second makes the choice immune to feedback.

A civilization can fail to adapt without lacking information. It only needs a
story in which the information that should change its mind can always be
dismissed.

## A local minimum in a moving landscape

The analogy that keeps coming to mind is gradient descent. Imagine an adaptive
system moving through a landscape in search of a lower loss. It makes local
improvements, follows the gradient it can observe, and eventually reaches a
point where nearby changes appear to make things worse. It stops. Relative to
its local surroundings, it has found a minimum.

But a local minimum is not a global minimum. More importantly, the landscape is
not fixed. The world changes the shape of the problem while the system is still
optimizing for the one it learned before.

Schematically, let the system's loss landscape depend on time:

$$
\mathcal{L}_t(x) \neq \mathcal{L}_{t+\Delta t}(x)
$$

A configuration that was locally successful under one environment may be a poor
position under another. The problem becomes worse if the system's update rule
treats departure from its current basin as a cost in itself. Reaching a better
region may require an interval of apparent regression: uncertainty,
reallocation, failure, and disruption before the benefits become visible.

This is not a mathematical model of Europe. A society has no single loss
function, and its citizens disagree—often reasonably—about what should be
optimized. The analogy is useful for a narrower reason: it separates being
locally stable from being adaptively capable. A system can become very good at
improving its current arrangement while losing the ability to notice when the
arrangement itself has become the problem.

Every society has equilibria. The question is whether the conditions that
rewarded the current one are still the conditions that will determine success.

## The advantage of what already works

There is a well-developed idea in organizational research that comes close to
this problem. In his 1991 paper *Exploration and Exploitation in Organizational
Learning*, James G. March distinguishes between refining what an organization
already knows and searching for new possibilities[^2]. Exploitation tends to
produce clearer short-term returns. Exploration is uncertain, expensive, and
often looks wasteful before it succeeds. An adaptive system can therefore become
increasingly effective in the short run while undermining itself over the long
run: it improves what it already knows faster than it learns what it does not
know.

A country can exhibit the same pattern without consciously deciding to stagnate.
Its institutions become good at delivering the outcomes around which they were
built. Companies learn how to comply with the rules. Voters become attached to
arrangements that work for them. Administrations add processes to prevent past
failures from recurring. Organized interests learn how to defend their place in
the system. Each step can be locally reasonable. Together, they can make
unfamiliar projects harder to attempt and unsuccessful arrangements harder to
abandon.

Over time, the capacity to exploit the existing model becomes more visible than
the capacity to explore alternatives. The former produces reliable services and
familiar forms of success. The latter initially produces failed companies,
contentious reforms, wasted investment, and projects that do not work. If the
political system sees those costs clearly but sees the cost of not trying only
much later, caution can win by default.

That is how stability can become a trap without anyone being foolish. The
selection pressure favors what can be defended now over what may become
necessary later.

## Europe can diagnose the problem. Can it move?

The economic evidence matters, but as a symptom and a constraint—not as a
complete definition of progress. According to the OECD's 2026 productivity
indicators, EU labour productivity was roughly 90% of the US level around 2000
and around 75% in 2024. In 2024, output per hour grew by 0.2% in the EU and 2.2%
in the United States[^3]. One year's growth rate is not a forecast, and
productivity is not a complete measure of a good life. Over a quarter-century,
however, relative divergence of this kind is difficult to explain away as a mere
difference in lifestyle.

The more revealing point is that the problem is not simply invention. An OECD
study published in October 2026 found that EU firms that patent can display
invention intensity comparable to US peers, yet take around 1.5 years longer to
reach scale after their first patent filing. The financing gap becomes
especially pronounced at later stages, when some US firms can raise much larger
rounds[^4]. This is a more precise description than saying Europe lacks talent
or ideas: it struggles to turn some capabilities into technologies and firms
that scale.

Mario Draghi's 2024 report on European competitiveness made a related argument.
Its concern was not that Europe had no scientific strength or successful
industries, but that new technologies were not translating into enough new
companies with the scale to reshape markets, while fragmented markets and
capital constrained commercialization[^5]. Europe can diagnose the problem. The
existence of a diagnosis does not prove that the system can execute the cure.

Nor is Europe one uniform case. Productivity outcomes differ among its
countries, and in 2024 some European economies grew much faster than others.
This variation matters: it argues against an essential claim about European
people or culture. The question is about recurring institutional patterns and
collective capacity, not a continent-wide personality trait.

## When values become a filter

The harder problem is the possibility of self-righteousness: a system comes to
interpret its own arrangement not simply as preferable, but as evidence of moral
or civilizational superiority. At that point, criticism from outside is no
longer treated first as information. It becomes a sign that the critic has
misunderstood what matters.

The United States can be dismissed as excessively commercial, unequal, or
socially harsh. China can be dismissed as authoritarian or politically
unacceptable. Those criticisms can be true. Neither country is a template Europe
should copy wholesale. But a criticism can be valid and still become a shield.
If every external success is explained away by the defects of the society that
produced it, then no external success can teach you anything.

The distinction is between rejecting an entire model and learning from a
particular capability. One can oppose the United States' health-care
arrangements and still study its ability to finance and scale technology firms.
One can reject China's political system and still study how it builds
infrastructure or coordinates industrial capacity. Learning from an outcome does
not require endorsing the society that produced it.

A system that cannot make this distinction has turned values into a filter that
screens out inconvenient evidence. It may still discuss competition,
productivity, and innovation. It may even produce excellent reports about them.
But the reports are absorbed into the existing worldview rather than allowed to
revise it.

This is not simple ignorance. The society may have enough information to
recognize a changing environment, while its shared explanations and incentive
structures make it difficult for that information to change behavior.

## Wealth makes the feedback late

Accumulated wealth can conceal the consequences of losing adaptive capacity. A
society built through generations of industrial expertise, infrastructure,
capital, and research does not become poor the moment its rate of innovation
slows. It can continue living on what was built before. Its cities remain
beautiful, its firms remain competent in established sectors, and its citizens
can experience real security even as its relative capacity to shape new
industries declines.

This creates an asymmetry between the time a cause appears and the time its cost
becomes undeniable. The loss of a future capability is difficult to observe
because the missing thing has not yet been needed. The comfort supported by
yesterday's capabilities is observable every day. The political system
consequently receives stronger feedback for preserving the present than for
preparing for an uncertain future.

By the time the gap becomes obvious in strategic dependence, technological
lock-in, or fiscal pressure, the missing skills, firms, capital markets, and
supply chains may take years to rebuild. Catch-up is possible, but it is not
free. Nor should it be assumed that every capability can be purchased from
abroad forever, particularly if it becomes central to security or economic
power.

Europe is not destined to be left behind in fifty years. That is not a forecast
anyone can make with confidence. The point is that relative capabilities can
compound, and a long delay between declining adaptability and visible hardship
can make a society less responsive precisely when response becomes more urgent.

## A good life is not the end of progress

There is a legitimate counterargument. Europeans may value different things:
more leisure, lower inequality, stronger protections, and less tolerance for
disruption. They may be willing to accept lower measured output in exchange.
That is a valid political choice if it is informed, sustainable, and revisable.

But revealed preference is not always as clear as it looks. A society may say it
prefers not to take risks after making risk-taking unusually difficult. It may
celebrate the absence of disruptive new firms after making it harder for such
firms to scale. It may call a lack of change a choice when that lack is partly
the consequence of lost capacity. Without a credible counterfactual, comfort
alone cannot tell us which story is true.

The question is not whether Europe should become America or China. Nor is it
whether every technology should be adopted, every regulation scrapped, or every
established industry sacrificed for a speculative future. It is whether Europe
can preserve the ability to choose differently as the world changes.

Adaptive capacity is measured by more than strategies, funds, or reports. Can
capital and talent move toward new opportunities? Can failed policies and firms
be abandoned without years of ritual? Can successful experiments scale across
borders? Can external evidence revise domestic assumptions? Can institutions
accept short-term costs before a long-term necessity becomes a crisis?

A society does not have to maximize growth to be successful. It does, however,
need enough capability to finance and revise the life it has chosen.

The danger is not that Europe has chosen a good life. It is that it may begin to
treat the good life it inherited as proof that it has reached the end of
history. A civilization's achievements should give it more freedom to explore,
not less reason to do so. The real test is whether, while its arrangements still
provide comfort and security, it can recognize when they are no longer
enough—and change before reality makes the decision for it.
