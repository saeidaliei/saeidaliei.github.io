---
title: "Rest in Peace Alan Turing"
date: 2026-11-15
---

There is something profoundly tragic about Alan Turing's life. 
We remember him as the father of computer science, the mathematician who helped break Enigma, 
and the man whose ideas laid the foundations of artificial intelligence. 
But the most interesting thing about Turing was not what he built, but what he was trying to understand.

He was, at heart, fascinated by the mind.

Turing became preoccupied with the relationship between mind and matter: 
What is a mind? How does it emerge from physical matter? What happens to it when the body dies? 
Could something as seemingly extraordinary as human thought ultimately be understood through mathematics and physical processes?

Another question that occupied him was free will. Influenced by the physicist Arthur Eddington and the emerging ideas of quantum mechanics, 
Turing considered whether the apparent unpredictability of the physical world might leave room for human freedom. 
In an unpublished early essay, *Nature of Spirit*, he explored the relationship between matter, spirit, and the will, 
even speculating that the mind might somehow influence physical processes in the brain.

He returned to this problem years later, now from the perspective of artificial intelligence. He articulated a fascinating contradiction: 
if a human brain behaves like a machine governed by physical laws, how can we reconcile that with our experience of free will? Perhaps, 
he suggested, our feeling of freedom is an illusion. Or perhaps we really do possess free will, 
but there is no way to establish this simply by observing our behaviour. He never definitively resolved the question. 
What makes this especially interesting is that he was already confronting a problem that remains central 
to debates about consciousness and artificial intelligence: if a machine can imitate all our outward behaviour, 
what, if anything, distinguishes its inner experience from ours?

These questions never really left him. They simply took different forms.

The universal machine he conceived in 1936 was not merely an abstract mathematical construction. 
It opened the possibility that a single machine could perform any computation expressible through precise instructions. 
After the war, he wanted to build such a machine, but his ambitions went considerably further. 
He wanted to understand whether machinery could learn, develop, and eventually exhibit the capacities we associate with intelligence
. He even spoke of *building a brain*.

In the late 1940s, Turing explored neurology, physiology, and what we would now recognise as neural networks. 
In 1950, he published *Computing Machinery and Intelligence*, 
asking the question that would define the field of artificial intelligence: Can machines think?

But there was another side to Turing that is often overlooked. He was deeply interested in biology. 
In his final years, he turned to morphogenesis: the mathematical principles governing how biological forms develop. 
He wanted to understand how patterns, structures, and asymmetries emerge in living organisms from seemingly simple underlying processes. 
His 1952 paper, *The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis*, remains a remarkable contribution to mathematical biology.

Looking at these apparently different pursuits together, a certain unity emerges. Turing was interested in the emergence of complexity: 
how matter gives rise to mind, how simple rules produce sophisticated behaviour, how a machine might learn, 
and how biological organisms acquire their forms. 
Computation, biology, and intelligence were different manifestations of questions that occupied the same mind.

In late 1948, Turing moved to Manchester, where he would spend the remaining six years of his life. 
He joined the University of Manchester as a mathematician and became Deputy Director of its Computing Machine Laboratory. 
It was here that some of his most ambitious ideas began to take practical shape. He worked on the programming of some of the 
earliest modern computers, explored the possibility of machine intelligence, and published his famous 1950 paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence. 
In a sense, Manchester became the place where his different intellectual obsessions converged: mathematics, computation, biology, and the mind.

And then there was the cruelty.

In 1952, Turing was prosecuted for homosexuality, which was criminalised in Britain at the time. 
The man who had helped save his country during the Second World War was convicted by that same country. 
He was given the choice of imprisonment or undergoing hormonal treatment. 
He accepted the latter and was subjected to injections of oestrogen, 
a process commonly described as chemical castration. 
His security clearance was withdrawn, and he lost access to government cryptographic work.

It is difficult to comprehend the injustice. A society, a world, that benefited enormously 
from his intellect could not tolerate the person he was.

On 7 June 1954, at just 41 years old, Turing died at his home in Wilmslow, Cheshire. 
He was found dead the following morning, having died from cyanide poisoning. 
The inquest returned a verdict of suicide. His unfinished scientific projects, 
his questions about intelligence, and his work on biological form were left behind. 
The British government would eventually apologise for its treatment of him in 2009, 
and he received a "royal pardon" in 2013 (so royal of them, I don't know who or what gave them the right to criminalize or pardon people). 
No apology, of course, could return the years or the life that had been taken from him.

And now, more than seventy years later, something extraordinary is happening.

The question Turing asked in 1950 is no longer a philosophical curiosity. 
Artificial intelligence is becoming a practical reality. 
Machines can learn from data, generate language, solve problems, recognise patterns, 
and perform increasingly sophisticated intellectual tasks, and one day even become conscious if not already.
Neural networks, which he explored long before they became central to computer science, are now at the heart of this transformation.

We have not solved consciousness. We do not know whether today's machines understand anything in the way humans do, 
or whether intelligence and subjective experience are as closely connected as we might imagine. 
But the territory Turing wanted to explore has become one of the most important scientific and technological frontiers of our time.

Perhaps this is the greatest irony of his life: the world that rejected him is now building the very things he spent his life imagining.

Turing wanted to understand the mind, not merely to calculate numbers. 
He wanted to understand intelligence as a phenomenon of nature, 
and whether its underlying principles could be realised in something other than biological tissue.

We are still pursuing that dream and we're very close.

Rest in peace, Alan Turing knowing that your suffering didn't go to waste.
