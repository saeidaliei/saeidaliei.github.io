Some lectures become familiar enough to turn into a soundtrack for sleep. 
Robert Sapolsky’s Stanford course on *Human Behavioral Biology* has accompanied countless nights of mine. 
That is not a complaint. Few courses reward repetition so well.

The central idea is deceptively simple: human behavior has no single cause.

We like explanations that stop somewhere. A person behaves aggressively because of their hormones, 
their upbringing, their genes, or the culture they grew up in. Each explanation may contain something true, 
but the trouble begins when we mistake one layer of a causal chain for the whole chain.

Sapolsky’s approach is to keep following it.

What happened in the seconds before an action? What about the minutes, hours, 
and days preceding it? What shaped the person’s brain during childhood, before birth, 
and across generations of evolutionary history? How do genes, hormones, neural circuits, 
social environments, and culture interact to produce a particular behavior?

These are not competing explanations. They are different parts of the same problem.

Across 25 lectures, originally recorded at Stanford in 2010, Sapolsky moves through evolution, 
genetics, neuroscience, endocrinology, aggression, sexuality, language, mental illness, and individual differences. 
The subjects are diverse, but the underlying discipline remains the same: 
resist the temptation to explain a complex phenomenon from within a single field.

The most valuable lesson is not that biology explains everything. 
It is that **an explanation becomes weaker when it stops looking for causes simply because it has found a plausible one.**

This also changes how we think about other people. 
Understanding the causes of a behavior does not automatically justify it, 
but it makes simplistic judgments harder to sustain. 
The person in front of us is not an isolated decision-maker who appeared fully formed at the moment of action. 
They are the current state of an extraordinarily complicated history.

The lectures are not a final theory of human nature, 
and individual scientific claims should not be treated as timeless. 
Their enduring value lies in the method: connect the levels, 
follow the causal chain, and be suspicious of explanations that are too comfortable.

The entire course is available for free on [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NNnIGh9g6fA&list=PL848F2368C90DDC3D).

I keep returning to it. Perhaps the most revealing thing about a good explanation is that, 
even after you know where it is going, you still find something worth following.
