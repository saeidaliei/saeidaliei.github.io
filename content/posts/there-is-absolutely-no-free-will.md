---
title: "There is Absolutely No Free Will"
date: 2026-10-19
---

There is a simple argument against free will, and it is uncomfortable mostly
because it is simple. Every event has a cause, or it does not. If it has a
cause, you did not author it. If it does not, you did not author it either.
There is no third option in which *you* appear as an uncaused cause, and nobody
has ever been able to say what that third option would physically be. Everything
else in this post is commentary on that paragraph.

The title is deliberately absolute, so let me say exactly what is being denied.
I am not denying that you deliberate, that you choose, that your choices matter,
or that you are a coherent agent. I am denying one specific thing:

> **That at the moment of choosing, you could have done otherwise, with the same
> past, the same laws, and the same brain, and that *you* are the ultimate
> source of which way it went.**

That is contra-causal free will. It is what most people mean when they are not
being careful, and it is what I think does not exist.

## The two horns

Take any decision, say reaching for the left cup instead of the right one.
Either the outcome was fixed by prior conditions, meaning the state of your
brain, which was fixed by your history, your genes, your environment and the
laws of physics,

$$
\text{state}_t \xrightarrow{\ \text{laws}\ } \text{state}_{t+1}
$$

or it was not fixed, in which case something in the process was random.
Determinism gives you a chain you did not choose, and randomness gives you a
coin flip you did not choose. A random event is not a free one: if a decision
came from a quantum fluctuation in a neuron, it is not *more* yours than one
that came from the neuron's prior state. It is less yours, and it is not even a
reason.

This is the structure of van Inwagen's consequence argument[^1] and of Galen
Strawson's Basic Argument[^2]. Strawson's version is the cleanest. To be truly
responsible for what you do, you would have to be responsible for the way you
are. To be responsible for the way you are, you would have to have chosen it,
and to have chosen it you would have needed to be some prior way that made you
choose it. That regress does not terminate. You did not pick your genes, your
parents, your language, your early childhood, or the order in which you met the
ideas that now feel like your own, and there is no point in the chain at which a
self steps in from outside it.

## Turtles all the way down

Robert Sapolsky has spent a career on the biology of behavior, and his argument
in *Behave* (2017)[^3] and then *Determined* (2023)[^4] is essentially this
regress filled in with neuroscience. Why did you do that? Because of the neurons
firing a second earlier. Those fired because of the sensory input and hormones
of the minutes before, which depended on your sleep, your hunger and your stress
that day, which depended on your childhood, which depended on your fetal
environment and your genes, which depended on your ancestors, the evolutionary
pressures on them, and the culture that shaped all of it. At no timescale does a
free agent show up.

The part I find most useful in *Determined* is not the chain itself but that
Sapolsky goes after the standard escape routes one by one: chaos, emergence and
quantum indeterminism[^4]. Chaotic systems are unpredictable in practice, not
undetermined in principle. Emergent levels of description are real, but they do
not escape the causal closure of the levels beneath them. And indeterminism, as
above, buys randomness rather than authorship. He also uses cases like Charles
Whitman, who in 1966 killed more than a dozen people from the University of
Texas tower and whose autopsy revealed a brain tumor pressing on the
amygdala[^4]. Most of us feel that this changes how we should think about his
responsibility, and Sapolsky's point is that it should change it *without a
threshold*. The tumor is simply the one cause we happened to find, and we
usually do not find the others.

## The view from the inside

Sam Harris makes a different and, I think, complementary argument in *Free Will*
(2012), and it is an introspective one[^5]. Watch your next thought arise. You
did not choose it, and you could not have, because you would have had to think
it first. Your intentions, your reasons and the sudden preference for one cup
over another all appear in consciousness, and consciousness is then
retrospectively told a story in which it authored them. The feeling of
authorship is real, but the authorship is not.

Harris also stresses something that tends to be underweighted: the argument is
not that you are being forced against your will, but that the will itself is one
more thing you find yourself with. You can be free from coercion without being
the author of the desires you are free to act on. This matters for what follows,
because it means the sense of freedom is evidence about the brain's model of
itself, not evidence about the causal structure of the world.

## What neuroscience adds, and what it does not

The experiments are usually presented as the heart of the case, and I think they
are not, so I want to be careful here. Libet and colleagues (1983)[^6] found
that a slow electrical build-up in the brain, the readiness potential, began
roughly half a second before a voluntary movement and well before subjects
reported being aware of the urge to move. Soon, Brass, Heinze and Haynes
(2008)[^7] decoded a left/right choice from prefrontal and parietal activity
several seconds before subjects reported deciding, though only modestly above
chance. Fried, Mukamel and Kreiman (2011)[^8] did something similar with single
neurons in human medial frontal cortex, with much higher accuracy at much
shorter lead times.

These results are suggestive, but they are also contested. Schurger, Sitt and
Dehaene (2012)[^9] argued that the readiness potential may simply reflect
ongoing random fluctuations crossing a threshold, rather than a decision already
made unconsciously, so reading the Libet result as proof of anything is too
strong. That is fine, because the argument above does not need it. What the
experiments establish, together with Daniel Wegner's work in *The Illusion of
Conscious Will* (2002)[^10], is something narrower and still important:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\text{the feeling of consciously initiating an act is a report} \\
\text{produced by the brain, and it can be wrong.}
\end{gathered}
$$

That is enough. The philosophy carries the conclusion, and the neuroscience only
removes the intuition that we would know it if we were not free.

## Physics does not rescue it

Laplace stated the classical picture in 1814[^11]: an intellect that knew all
forces and positions at one time could compute the entire future. Classical
mechanics is deterministic, and nothing in it has a place for an uncaused
chooser. Quantum mechanics is the usual hope, because it appears to be
indeterministic, but notice what we get if we take that at face value.
Randomness at the level of ion channels is noise, and noise does not make a
decision more yours. Whether quantum mechanics is fundamentally random is also
interpretation-dependent, since the Schrödinger evolution is deterministic and
interpretations such as Everett's[^12] and Bohm's[^13] keep it that way. Either
way the structure of the first section stands. Einstein did not like dice, but
the deeper point is that even a universe that does play dice does not thereby
produce a self that rolls them.

## Our own work: why it feels free

So there is a puzzle. If the argument is this short, why does the feeling of
freedom survive it? I think the model we developed in [Cognition as
Non-Commutative Geometry](https://saeidaliei.github.io/posts/ncg-cognition)[^14]
offers a structural answer, and I want to state carefully what it does and does
not claim, because it would be easy to abuse. In that post the proposal is that
a sufficiently self-referential cognitive system should be modeled as a
non-commutative algebra of operations \$\mathcal A\$ together with a state
\$\omega\$ on it. The central step was that self-reference makes operations
order-sensitive, because the system's act of inspecting itself changes the state
it is inspecting:

$$
AB \neq BA.
$$

What this does not do is introduce indeterminism. Non-commutativity is a
statement about order, not about chance, and the dynamics generated by the pair
\$(\mathcal A,\omega)\$, the modular flow

$$
\sigma_t^\omega(A) = \Delta_\omega^{it} A \Delta_\omega^{-it},
$$

is a one-parameter automorphism group, which is as deterministic as anything
gets. Given the algebra and the state, the flow is fixed. So the model gives no
loophole for free will, and that is a feature rather than a failure.

What it does do is explain the shape of the illusion. A system that contains a
model of itself, \$M(S)\subseteq S\$, has a particular problem: each time the
model is applied, it changes the state it was meant to describe,

$$
S \xrightarrow{A} S', \qquad M(S) \text{ must now describe } S'.
$$

The self-model is always one step behind the system it models, so from the
inside there is no vantage point from which the next state of the system is
fully available before it happens. This is not Gödel's theorem and I would not
say it is, but it is structurally the same family of limits on self-description.
The consequence is that the system cannot, from inside, predict its own next
decision in the way an outside observer with the full state could. From the
inside the future looks open: epistemically open and ontologically closed.

This is an old idea in a new vocabulary. Donald MacKay made the same point in
1960 in *On the logical indeterminacy of a free choice*[^15], arguing that a
deterministic brain's own state cannot be fully predicted by that brain in a way
it is bound to accept, and the halting problem[^16] teaches the same lesson in
computation: a perfectly deterministic program can be impossible to shortcut, so
that the only way to know what it does is to run it. Running it, from the
inside, is called deliberating. The feeling of free will is what
unpredictability-from-within looks like from within.

There is a second consequence worth noting. In the NCG picture learning is not
only a change of state, \$\omega\to\omega'\$, but also a change of algebra,
\$\mathcal A\to\mathcal A'\$, meaning a change in which operations are available
at all. A person who has learned to reason recursively, or to speak a second
language, or to recognize a pattern of manipulation, has a different space of
possible actions than before. This is the part of "freedom" that is real. It is
not the ability to step outside the causal chain but the growth of the set of
operations the chain can run through, a quantity that can be larger or smaller,
can be nurtured or destroyed, and matters morally and politically a great deal.

All of this is conjecture, in the same sense as the original post. The
identification of that mathematics with cognition is the hypothesis, and what I
am claiming here is only the conditional: *if* cognition has that structure, the
sense of free will is expected, and the fact of free will is not.

## Spinoza had it

None of this is new, and the person who said it most cleanly had no neuroscience
at all. In the *Ethics* (1677)[^17], Spinoza argued that people think themselves
free because they are conscious of their actions and ignorant of the causes that
determine them. In his letter to Schuller[^18] he gave the image of a stone set
in motion that, if it could think, would believe it was moving of its own will.
Spinoza did not conclude that nothing matters. He concluded that freedom needs
to be redefined: freedom is not the absence of necessity but acting from your
own nature rather than from external compulsion, and more than that,
understanding the causes that move you. That is the only freedom I think exists,
and it comes in degrees.

It is also the notion that matters when we ask what it would take for any mind,
biological or artificial, to be free, and it is the thread connecting this post
to [the Visprax
announcement](https://saeidaliei.github.io/posts/visprax-unsafeai-announcement)[^19].
A free mind is not an uncaused mind. It is a mind whose actions flow from its
own structure, and that understands that structure.

## What changes, and what does not

What does change is how we treat people who cause harm, and I think it changes
almost everything. If nobody ultimately authors their character, then there is
no threshold at which someone becomes deserving of suffering, because it is
turtles all the way down for the person who steals and for the person who kills.
The tumor in Whitman’s brain is just the cause we happened to find, and there
are always others we haven’t. Seen this way, imprisonment as we practice it,
with its aim of making someone pay for what they did, makes no sense to me, and
I think future generations will look at it the way we look at the stocks and the
dungeon: as something medieval, built on a picture of the person that biology
has made untenable. The one thing that survives is the narrow case where someone
is an active danger to others and has to be kept apart from them, which is what
Sapolsky calls the quarantine model[^4][^20]. That is a very different thing
from prison. We keep a child with a contagious illness at home without hatred,
without a sentence, and with a clear expectation that they will rejoin everyone
once they’re well, and the same logic would demand that any restraint be as
limited, humane and temporary as possible, paired with real effort to understand
and treat the causes. Almost everything else, the punishment, the stigma, the
lifelong record, the revenge dressed up as justice, falls away once you take the
argument seriously. We won’t stop feeling the pull toward retribution, since it
is an old emotion that served social species well, but we can stop building
institutions on it.

## The strongest objections

A post with this title should say where it is most likely to be wrong. The first
objection is **compatibilism**, the majority position among professional
philosophers: in the 2020 PhilPapers survey[^21], roughly three in five accepted
or leaned toward compatibilism, around a fifth toward libertarianism, and only
about one in nine toward no free will. Dennett (*Elbow Room*, 1984[^22];
*Freedom Evolves*, 2003[^23]) and Frankfurt (1969)[^24] argue that what we care
about in free will, acting on our own reasons, without coercion, and responsive
to reasons, is fully compatible with determinism, and that demanding
contra-causal freedom is demanding something incoherent and then complaining
that it is missing. I think this is the most serious opposing view. My reply is
that it changes the subject: the compatibilist freedom is real, it is close to
Spinoza's, and I have used it above, but it does not give what ordinary talk of
desert and ultimate responsibility demands, and the honest thing is to say so
rather than keep the word. Compatibilists would answer that "free will" in the
everyday sense never meant the contra-causal thing and that I am the one
redefining it, which is a real disagreement about what the words refer to and
not only about the physics.

The second is **libertarianism**. Robert Kane (*The Significance of Free Will*,
1996)[^25] and agent-causal theorists such as Chisholm[^26] argue that
indeterminism at the right moments in the right neural processes, or a distinct
kind of causation exercised by agents, can supply genuine authorship. The
standard worry is the one from the start of this post: it is unclear how an
undetermined event can be more controlled, and agent causation seems to posit a
causal power with no physical account. The third is the **experiments**, which,
as noted, are contested, though nothing above depends on them. The last is **my
own model**. The NCG framework is a conjecture, and the step that matters here,
that self-reference yields a structural gap between self-model and system, would
need to be tested and could fail. If it does, the feeling of freedom still needs
some other explanation, but the argument of the first sections does not depend
on it.

## What would change my mind

It is worth saying what evidence would count. I would need an account of a
physical event in a brain that is neither fixed by prior states nor random, and
that is nonetheless attributable to a *self* in a way that makes the self
responsible for it. Not a gesture toward one, but an account. I do not think
this can be provided, because the two categories, caused and uncaused, exhaust
the possibilities and neither one is authorship. But that is exactly the kind of
claim a counterexample would refute, and I would be happy to be shown one.

## The actual claim

Put compactly, caused or uncaused, neither gives ultimate authorship. The
feeling of freedom is real: it is produced by a system that cannot fully see
itself from within, and that therefore experiences its own next state as open.
The usefulness of freedom is also real, in the form of the expanding set of
operations a mind can perform and the understanding of the causes that move it.
But the metaphysical freedom, a self standing outside the causal chain and
steering it, does not exist. There is no free will, and there is still a you. It
is just not the thing you thought was steering.

## Scope and limits

This post argues against one specific thing, contra-causal free will, and it
does not claim that deliberation, choice or agency are illusions. The title is
intentionally stronger than the weakest version of the claim, but not stronger
than the argument. I do not think we can prove that no physical process could
ever underwrite authorship. I think no one can say what such a process would be,
and that this is the same thing.

The neuroscience is not load-bearing. The Libet-style experiments are contested,
and the argument in the first sections stands or falls on the logic of caused
versus uncaused, not on any readiness potential. The proportions I give from the
2020 PhilPapers survey are approximate readings of its free will question, and
since respondents could endorse more than one answer they should not be read as
precise.

The section on our own work is a conjecture in the same sense as the original
post. It supports a conditional only: if cognition has the structure proposed
there, then the feeling of free will is expected without the fact of it. It does
not establish that cognition has that structure. The paragraph on punishment is
also a different kind of claim from the rest. It is a normative extension of the
argument that I find compelling, and it does not follow from the metaphysics
alone. Sapolsky's quarantine model is paraphrased from his public statements,
and people who accept the argument about free will can still disagree about
deterrence, incapacitation and what institutions should look like in practice.
