There is an obvious image of coercion: a locked door, a direct threat, a hand
preventing someone from leaving. But control does not always need a visible
barrier. It can operate through the relationships a person depends on, the
reputation they carry, the things they hope for, and the social environment that
tells them what they are permitted to feel or want.

The events motivating this essay are not hypothetical. The harder question is
how to understand their structure: how individual acts connect, how humiliation
and attachment can reinforce one another, and how a social environment can
normalize harm by describing it as discipline, protection, correction, or even
help.

My central claim is that these patterns cannot always be understood by looking
at a single incident or a single relationship. In some cases, three layers must
be examined together: **the intimate bond, the social environment, and the story
that makes the treatment appear legitimate**. The first can make a person
emotionally dependent; the second can deprive them of independent support and
reality-checking; the third can turn the resulting suffering into something the
target is told they deserve.

This is not a claim that every abusive relationship involves a coordinated
group, or that every instance of shame is a form of coercive control. It is a
way of analyzing a pattern when fear, exclusion, attachment, and the systematic
erosion of a person's agency occur together.

## Coercion is a pattern, not merely an incident

A model of abuse built only around discrete acts of physical violence misses a
large part of how people can be controlled. A person may have no chain on their
body and still find that their movements, relationships, choices, and sense of
what is possible have narrowed. They may technically be free to leave while
facing the loss of their friends, reputation, livelihood, family relationships,
or only source of emotional relief.

Evan Stark's account of *coercive control* is useful here because it shifts
attention from isolated incidents to a pattern that can combine intimidation,
isolation, surveillance, and the deprivation of ordinary liberty[^6]. The key
question is not only, “What did this person do?” It is also, “What has the
person been made unable to do, and what happens when they try?”

Gradual escalation matters in this kind of pattern. Control may begin with
behavior that looks small enough to excuse: a slight insult, an intrusive
request, a demand framed as concern, or a rule said to apply only in special
circumstances. Over time, the boundary moves. What would once have been
unacceptable becomes familiar; what was familiar becomes compulsory; and the
cost of refusing grows as the target loses resources and outside relationships.

The pattern can therefore become coercive before it looks dramatic from the
outside. The target has been adapting to each local change, while the cumulative
structure is changing the conditions under which they can choose.

## Public shaming turns the social world into an instrument

Humiliation is not only an unpleasant feeling. When it is repeated, staged, or
amplified by other people, it can change the target's relationship to the
community in which they must live. The person is no longer responding only to
the original act. They must also anticipate who has heard about it, who believes
it, who will repeat it, who will withdraw from them, and what the next social
consequence might be.

This is one reason public shaming can work as a form of control. It makes the
audience part of the punishment. A colleague's silence, a relative's withdrawal,
a neighbor's contempt, or a group's refusal to hear an explanation may each
appear minor when isolated. Together, such responses can produce a practical
form of exclusion: fewer people to consult, fewer places to feel safe, and fewer
independent accounts against which to check the version of events being imposed.

Research on social ostracism has found that exclusion can affect how people
perceive their own humanity and the humanity of those excluding them. Bastian
and Haslam's experiments found that ostracized participants tended to judge
themselves and their ostracizers as less human, with aspects of ordinary “human
nature” especially sensitive to exclusion.[^7] That finding does not prove that
every real-world campaign of humiliation has the same cause or outcome. It does
help explain why social rejection can reach deeper than wounded pride: it can
disturb the person's sense of belonging to the human community itself.

The harm can become self-reinforcing. Isolation makes it harder to correct false
or distorted narratives; the absence of visible support is then interpreted as
evidence that the person deserves exclusion. The group treats the target's
isolation as confirmation of its own judgment, even when the isolation is partly
the result of the group's treatment.

## Weaponized attachment: when intimacy becomes a control surface

Traumatic bonding is commonly described as a strong emotional bond that can form
between an abused person and the person abusing them. Two important features in
the literature are a power imbalance and the intermittent rewards and punishment[^1][^5]. 
The attachment is not evidence that the harm was acceptable, nor that the person 
experiencing it secretly wanted to be harmed. It can arise under conditions 
designed to make safety unpredictable and emotional relief scarce.

The more recent concept of **weaponized attachment** shifts the analytic focus
toward what the perpetrator does with the bond. Rather than viewing attachment
only as a reaction occurring inside the victim, it asks whether closeness itself
has been cultivated and then used to sustain control. In their 2025 study, Mags
Lesiak and Loraine Gelsthorpe describe how grooming, disclosure of trauma,
emotional closeness, and alternating cruelty with care can form part of a
coercive pattern[^4]. Their study was qualitative and involved interviews with
18 women who had experienced repeated domestic abuse. It is not a universal
prevalence study, but it provides a direct account of how a perpetrator's
conduct can shape the attachment that later appears, from the outside, to be the
victim's inexplicable choice.

This perspective connects to the argument developed in my earlier post, *There
is Trauma Bond and Then There is Weaponized Attachment*, which reproduces Mags
Lesiak's discussion and points to the broader framework[^2]. The relevant
distinction is between attachment as a human capacity and attachment as an
instrument. The capacity to bond is not the
defect. The problem is a relationship in which closeness is used to destabilize
a person's ability to judge danger, retain autonomy, and leave.

The sequence can be especially confusing when the same source appears to offer
both the injury and the relief. Affection can seem to confirm that the
relationship is meaningful; its withdrawal can feel like proof that the target
has failed; the return of affection can then be experienced as rescue. Each
cycle can deepen the wish to recover the earlier relationship, even when the
pattern itself is causing the harm.

This is also why “Why don't they just leave?” is such a poor diagnostic
question. It treats the attachment as a free-standing preference, separate from
the power relations, fear, intermittent relief, and social costs surrounding it.
A better question is: **what has this relationship trained the person to expect,
fear, excuse, and hope for?**

## The next step: contaminating the future

The pattern becomes more destructive when it reaches beyond attachment to a
person and begins to affect the target's relationship with their own desires. A
goal, place, experience, or imagined future can become associated with
humiliation or betrayal until the original desire no longer feels available in
its former form.

In the events that motivate this essay, the issue was not limited to a bond
between two people. A destination that had once represented a hoped-for
experience could become tied to a distressing sexual or relational association
involving a third party. The destination remained physically available, but its
meaning had changed. What had once been an invitation to the future became a
reminder of violation, comparison, or loss.

It is tempting to call this jealousy or ordinary heartbreak and stop there. That
description may capture part of the feeling while missing the structure of the
injury. The deeper question is whether a person's independent sources of
anticipation are repeatedly being converted into sources of distress. If a
person loses not only a relationship but also the pleasures, plans, places,
interests, and goals that gave them a life beyond that relationship, the damage
is no longer confined to one attachment.

A useful descriptive phrase for this process is **associative devaluation**:
something once valued becomes linked with an aversive meaning and is
consequently harder to approach. The phrase names a possible process; by itself,
it does not establish intent or prove that every changed preference has been
manipulated. The context matters. A loss of interest can result from grief,
depression, stress, or many other causes. It becomes especially concerning when
it occurs as part of a wider pattern of intimidation, humiliation, control, or
repeated interference with the person's independent life.

The result can be a form of internal isolation. Even when the controlling person
is absent, the target may find that their own memories and preferences have
become difficult places to rest. The external restrictions have begun to
reproduce themselves inside the person's relationship with the world.

## Fear and shame can be sold as help

Coercive systems rarely describe themselves in their own language as coercive.
They may call their actions protection, tough love, purification,
rehabilitation, discipline, character-building, spiritual guidance, or a
necessary intervention. A target's resistance can then be presented as proof
that the intervention is needed: the person is said to be too proud, too
damaged, too irrational, too impure, or too attached to understand what is
supposedly being done for them.

Some high-control religious, ideological, or social groups use fear of
exclusion, moral condemnation, confession, surveillance, and conditional
belonging to maintain conformity. This is not an argument that religion as a
whole is coercive. Religious life can provide meaning, consolation, community,
and care. The relevant question is what happens when an institution or group
makes belonging contingent on obedience and casts disagreement as evidence of
moral or personal defect.

The same structure appears outside religion. A powerful group can describe
humiliation as a lesson, the destruction of a person's support network as
protection, or public condemnation as accountability. The vocabulary of help
creates a moral cover for conduct that would otherwise be recognized as cruelty.

Albert Bandura's work on moral disengagement helps explain how this cover can
function. People may redescribe harmful conduct in morally acceptable language,
minimize its consequences, shift responsibility to authorities or other group
members, and blame or dehumanize the people harmed[^8]. These mechanisms do not
require every participant to know the full pattern or share the same motive.
Responsibility can be distributed across separate acts until each participant
sees only a small, defensible part of the whole.

This is where plausible deniability matters. An act is explained away as a
misunderstanding; a humiliating pattern is reframed as concern; an inconsistency
becomes the target's failure to understand; responsibility is passed between
people until nobody appears accountable. The system remains able to produce a
cumulative outcome while denying that anyone intended it.

A claim of benevolent intent is therefore not enough to evaluate an
intervention. The harder tests are behavioral: Does it preserve the person's
autonomy? Can they refuse without retaliation? Can they consult people outside
the group? Are the rules transparent and consistent? Is the alleged helper
accountable for harm? If the answer to these questions is consistently no, the
language of help deserves to be examined rather than accepted at face value.

## Cults and collective weaponized attachment

The logic described above can extend beyond an intimate relationship. In a
high-control group, attachment may be distributed across a leader, a community,
a doctrine, a moral identity, a family network, and a promise of belonging. The
person may risk not one relationship but an entire social and existential world
by dissenting or leaving. This is one way to understand the collective dimension
of weaponized attachment: the bond is reinforced not only by one person, but by
the network of relationships and meanings surrounding that person.

This does not mean that religion itself, or every close-knit community, is
coercive. Unusual beliefs are not sufficient evidence of abuse. The more useful
question is how a group operates: whether authority is concentrated and
unaccountable, whether independent information and outside relationships are
restricted, whether dissent brings punishment, and whether belonging is made
conditional on obedience. Philip Zimbardo's overview of extreme social influence
describes how conformity, compliance, guilt, fear, identification, authority,
and social isolation can interact to constrain freedom of choice and action[^9].

The same practices can also connect individual attachment to collective
pressure. Affection and recognition may be granted when a member conforms, while
shame, confession, denunciation, or shunning may follow disagreement. Public
humiliation then becomes more than an interpersonal event: the audience helps
enforce the group's boundaries. Members can be invited to interpret the
punishment as moral correction, protection, or evidence that the excluded person
is defective. The resulting isolation can then be presented as confirmation of
the original accusation.

The International Cultic Studies Association cautions against assuming that all
high-control experiences are alike and emphasizes the possible effects of such
environments on autonomy, identity, cognition, relationships, and trust.[^10]
This is an important distinction. The aim is not to classify every demanding
belief system as a cult, but to examine observable practices and their effects:
Can members question authority without retaliation? Can they maintain outside
relationships and access independent information? Can they leave without
threats, coercive penalties, or the deliberate destruction of their social
world? Are leaders and institutions accountable for the harms they cause?

Under these conditions, control can become distributed. One person may define
the rules, others may repeat them, relatives may withdraw contact, and peers may
carry out social punishment. These participants need not all share one motive or
know the full consequences for their actions to combine into an environment that
constrains the target. Bandura's account of moral disengagement helps explain
how harmful conduct can be recast as duty, discipline, or protection, while
responsibility is displaced or minimized[^8]. Whether coordination or deliberate
intent occurred in a particular case remains a question for evidence, not
something that follows automatically from the cumulative effect.

In this article, the application of weaponized attachment to cultic or
high-control settings is an analytical extension, not a claim that it is a
separate clinical diagnosis or that every group follows the same mechanism. It
highlights a structural possibility: when belonging, identity, protection, and
meaning are all made dependent on the same authority, social attachment itself
can become an instrument of governance. Leaving then threatens not only a bond,
but the network that gave the bond its significance.

## From demoralization to dehumanization

Demoralization is often treated as a mood: discouragement, defeat, low
motivation. But in the pattern considered here, it can also describe the
cumulative effect of losing the conditions that make action feel worthwhile. The
target is exposed to fear, stripped of reliable support, shamed in front of
others, and repeatedly taught that their desires lead to humiliation or danger.
Eventually, the future itself may stop presenting itself as a place in which
anything good can happen.

The loss of desire is not the same thing as peaceful non-attachment. A person
can freely relinquish an ambition and still remain curious, connected, and
capable of choosing something else. It is different when a person feels that
wanting is unsafe, that every attachment can be turned against them, or that
every attempt to rebuild a private life will produce another punishment. This is
not liberation from desire. It is a narrowing of the space in which desire can
exist without fear.

When this extends to identity, the person may begin to feel less human, unreal,
numb, or detached from other people. These experiences do not mean the person is
less human. They are ways a person may experience severe distress,
disconnection, or sustained exclusion. The research on ostracism provides one
window into the relationship between social exclusion and self-dehumanization;
trauma-bonding research provides another into the way attachment, shame, and
power can become entangled[^1][^7]

Dehumanization also runs in the other direction. A group may first describe its
target as defective, dangerous, corrupt, or unworthy, making harsher treatment
seem acceptable. Over time, the target may internalize that description. The
group can then point to the person's distress, withdrawal, confusion, or anger
as further evidence that the original condemnation was right.

This is a closed loop: the treatment creates the very state that is then used to
justify the treatment. That loop is one of the reasons analysis must examine
sequences and social conditions, not just the final behavior of the person who
has been harmed.

## Why the social setting matters

A person can be harmed by one perpetrator. A social setting can make that harm
harder to recognize, harder to escape, and easier to repeat. Friends, relatives,
colleagues, religious authorities, professionals, and online audiences may each
have different information and different reasons for staying silent. Some may
knowingly assist; others may repeat a story, enforce a norm, or withdraw support
without understanding the larger consequences.

The distinction matters. It is not necessary to assume that every bystander is
part of a centrally coordinated plan to recognize that distributed behavior can
have a cumulative, controlling effect. Nor does the visible agreement of a group
establish that its account of the target is correct. Group consensus can reflect
shared evidence, but it can also reflect conformity, dependence, fear, repeated
messaging, or the absence of safe ways to disagree.

The practical test is to look at the pattern without allowing a single
explanation to swallow every fact. Record what occurred, when it occurred, what
was directly observed, what was communicated by whom, and what remains an
inference. Seek independent perspectives where possible. Distinguish a
demonstrated connection between events from a connection that is plausible but
not yet established. This is not a concession to the people who dismiss the
account; it is how an account becomes more precise and more difficult to
distort.

Taking someone's account seriously and examining its evidence are not opposing
acts. The first prevents reflexive dismissal. The second protects both the
person telling the story and the analysis from being trapped inside an
explanation that cannot be checked.

## What a more adequate framework would ask

If we treat coercion as a pattern rather than an incident, several questions
become central:

1. **Attachment:** Is closeness being used to create dependency, disorientation,
   or compliance? Are affection and hostility alternating in a way that makes
   the target continually seek relief from the same source of distress?
2. **Social environment:** Has the person lost access to independent
   relationships, information, resources, or spaces where their account can be
   heard without punishment?
3. **Shame and reputation:** Is public humiliation being used to narrow their
   choices, make them appear undeserving of support, or discourage others from
   helping?
4. **Meaning and desire:** Are interests, goals, places, or valued relationships
   repeatedly becoming associated with distress, and does that occur in the same
   broader pattern of pressure and control?
5. **Legitimation:** Is the treatment being presented as help, discipline,
   morality, rehabilitation, or protection? Can that claim withstand ordinary
   questions about consent, proportionality, transparency, and accountability?
6. **Responsibility:** Who makes decisions, who benefits, who is permitted to
   explain what happened, and who is expected to absorb the consequences?

No single answer proves the entire pattern. But these questions move the
analysis beyond the misleading idea that a person is simply too attached, too
sensitive, too ashamed, or too weak to leave. They direct attention to the
structure within which the person's responses have developed.

## Conclusion: the objective is not only compliance

The most obvious form of domination seeks obedience: make someone perform an
act, accept a rule, or remain in a relationship. A more extensive form can reach
beyond behavior and begin to alter the person's relationship with the sources of
their own meaning. Relationships become unsafe; social belonging becomes
conditional; desire becomes a liability; and the future becomes difficult to
imagine without anticipating humiliation.

That is why trauma bonds, weaponized attachment, public shaming, fear-based
control, and dehumanization belong in the same discussion. They are not
identical concepts, and one should not be used as proof of the others. But they
can describe different layers of a single destructive pattern: attachment can
bind the person, shame can isolate them, a legitimizing story can excuse the
treatment, and repeated loss can demoralize them until the world feels smaller
than it once did.

The corrective is not to demand that the target become more resilient while
leaving the structure untouched. It is to restore the conditions under which
independent judgment and attachment can function: safety without submission,
relationships without coercive debt, community without public degradation, and
the ability to want something without fearing that the desire itself will be
used against them.

The fundamental question is not why someone failed to remain untouched by such a
system. It is what kind of system makes a person's capacity for connection,
dignity, and future-directed desire into instruments of control—and what
safeguards are needed to prevent that from being treated as normal.

## Scope and limits

This essay connects several frameworks to analyze a pattern of events. It does
not claim that traumatic bonding, weaponized attachment, public shaming,
coercive control, and dehumanization are interchangeable terms. The Lesiak and
Gelsthorpe study is a qualitative study of 18 women reporting repeat domestic
abuse; it supports analysis of the perpetrator's role in attachment but does not
establish the prevalence of every pattern discussed here. Likewise, research on
ostracism and moral disengagement helps explain relevant mechanisms but cannot,
by itself, verify the motive behind any particular event. The argument is
strongest when it remains attentive to both the lived sequence of events and the
evidence supporting each causal connection.
