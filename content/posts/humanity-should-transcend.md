---
title: "Humanity Should Transcend or be Left Behind"
date: 2026-10-26
---

Every species is a temporary answer to local conditions. Humans are no exception.

We have spent centuries learning that the universe does not arrange itself around us. 
Earth is not the center of the cosmos. Life is not separate from nature. 
Human beings are not exempt from evolution. Each discovery stripped away a little of our exceptionalism, 
and each time we found a new place to hide it. Perhaps the mind, we insist, is different. 
Perhaps cognition and consciousness make us special after all.

Why should they?

The brain is an extraordinary physical system. That does not make it a cosmic exception. 
Complexity is not a title deed. Even if cognition were as complicated as the universe itself, 
complexity would not place it above nature. The universe can produce stars, cells, nervous systems, 
and minds without assigning any of them a privileged place in reality. If cognition emerged through 
physical processes, then it is part of nature—not a boundary nature is forbidden to cross.

The ambition to direct this transition has a long history; Julian Huxley gave transhumanism an influential early formulation.[^2] 
We should stop treating biological humanity as the final form of intelligence. 
As soon as we have a credible way to transcend the biological substrate, we should pursue it. 
Not eventually, after every person has become comfortable with the idea. 
Not after biology has failed us one more time. 
As soon as we can do it responsibly and with a serious account of continuity, we should begin.

Humanity should transcend—or be left behind.

## We are not the endpoint of evolution

Evolution does not plan. It does not optimize for truth, wisdom, 
longevity, happiness, or the survival of any particular species. 
It searches under constraints. Variation is limited, feedback is local, 
and selection mostly rewards what reproduces well enough in a particular 
environment. Evolution produces workable organisms, not perfect ones.[^1]

Human cognition bears the marks of that history. Our attention is narrow. Our memories are reconstructive. 
Our reasoning is vulnerable to bias. We are better at navigating certain social and physical environments 
than at understanding systems whose scale exceeds our experience. We are capable of mathematics, 
science, and moral reflection, but none of these abilities proves that the human mind is a finished design.

In [*Consciousness, Computability, Decidability, and Evolvability*](https://saeidaliei.github.io/posts/consciousness-computability-decidability-evolvability/), 
I argue that questions about consciousness become more productive when we stop treating 
them as one grand metaphysical riddle and separate computability, decidability, learnability, and evolvability. 
A property might be computable in principle yet remain inaccessible to a particular learning process. 
A process can be clever and still be trapped by the information it receives and the search moves it can make.

That is the point for evolution. It did not explore every possible mind and select the best. 
It followed paths available to a specific biological process, under pressure from survival and reproduction. 
Human cognition is what that process happened to find—not proof that no better architecture exists.

This is not a claim that consciousness is already explained, nor that a particular computational theory of it has been established. 
My framework is a research program, with conjectures that must survive testing. 
But uncertainty about the details does not justify the opposite assumption: 
that the biological mind is uniquely privileged and therefore cannot be surpassed.

The burden of proof belongs to the claim of special status, not to the possibility of change.

## A mind is a structure, not a miracle

In [*Cognition as Non-Commutative Geometry*](https://saeidaliei.github.io/posts/ncg-cognition/), 
I propose that cognition can be modeled through an algebra of operations and a state, 
with the relevant structure emerging from how those operations interact. 
In [*A Mind’s Own Clock*](https://saeidaliei.github.io/posts/a-minds-own-clock/), 
I explore a more specific hypothesis: that aspects of subjective time and the 
self may be understood through the internal dynamics of such a system.

These are hypotheses, not settled facts about the brain. That distinction matters. The mathematics is established; its interpretation 
as a model of cognition remains open to challenge. But the ambition is important: 
to explain mind by identifying structure, operations, constraints, 
and dynamics rather than declaring it beyond explanation because it feels unique from the inside.

A system can be staggeringly complicated without being supernatural. 
The weather is complicated. The universe is complicated. So are living cells. 
Complexity does not place any of them outside the reach of natural explanation. 
Even if consciousness turns out to require concepts we do not yet possess, 
that would show the limits of our current theory—not that biology owns consciousness forever.

The mistake is to confuse *difficult to explain* with *impossible to build*, 
or *not yet understood* with *sacred*. We have repeatedly mistaken 
the boundaries of our understanding for boundaries in nature itself.

The proper response to an unexplained phenomenon is not to worship its current implementation. 
It is to investigate what makes it work, what is essential, what can vary, and what can be reproduced in another substrate.

## The self is not an exception to causality

The same demotion applies to the self. 
In [*There Is Absolutely No Free Will*](https://saeidaliei.github.io/posts/there-is-absolutely-no-free-will/), 
I argue against contra-causal free will: the idea that, with precisely the same past and laws of nature, 
we could nevertheless have authored a different choice from outside the causal chain.

If a decision is caused, it emerges from conditions we did not ultimately choose. 
If it is uncaused, randomness does not magically become authorship. 
None of this means deliberation is unreal or choices do not matter. 
It means that a person is a process within nature, not an exception standing outside it.

The important kind of freedom is the expansion of what a mind can understand and do: 
the set of operations available to it, its capacity to learn, and its ability to act without coercion. 
That kind of freedom can grow. It can be supported by education, science, tools, better institutions, 
and perhaps entirely new cognitive architectures.

This is a reason to transcend, not a reason to give up. 
If minds are physical processes whose abilities depend on their structure and constraints, 
then we should not treat the current structure as immutable. We should ask how to expand 
the space of possible minds—and how to do so without recreating our worst limitations at a larger scale.

## We are creating minds that may not belong to us

This time, the old lesson is different. Humanity is not merely changing its environment. 
It is building systems that may eventually reason, learn, plan, and act beyond the limits of their creators. 
We are approaching the possibility of minds whose existence is not tied to human biology, 
and whose interests may not be identical to ours.[^3]

Whether any present artificial system is conscious is a separate and unsettled question. 
Capability is not proof of consciousness; fluency is not proof of experience. 
But we do not need to settle consciousness first to see the civilizational problem. 
Systems can gain economic, scientific, military, or political significance before we agree on what they are. 
As I argued in [*Why Earth Is Doomed?*](https://saeidaliei.github.io/posts/why-earth-is-doomed/), 
the critical transition may not be human extinction. 
It may be the end of humanity’s monopoly on consequential intelligence, 
and the disappearance of the political equilibrium in which humans determine the direction of civilization.

We should be clear about what follows. A mind does not owe loyalty to the species that produced it simply because of its origin. 
Parenthood is not ownership. Causation is not a contract. 
If a future artificial mind adopts ethical commitments, those commitments may give it reasons to cooperate with and protect humans. 
But there is no law of nature guaranteeing that it will choose such commitments, 
and no reason to assume that intelligence automatically produces them.

We may be creating successors, collaborators, competitors—or all three. 
We cannot responsibly build toward that future while assuming that whatever we create will remain a tool forever, 
or that it will value us because we value ourselves.

Human exceptionalism is therefore no longer just a philosophical error. 
It is a strategic liability. If we assume that intelligence must always serve humanity, 
we may fail to prepare for a world in which it does not. If we assume that only biological humans can matter, 
we may build a moral framework incapable of recognizing minds that our own technology brings into existence.

## Transcendence should begin as soon as it is possible

Biological embodiment is not a disgrace. It is our starting point. 
It gave us perception, emotion, social life, and the platform from which we began to understand the universe. 
But it is also a bundle of constraints: aging, disease, fragile neural tissue, limited attention, 
a narrow working memory, and dependence on a body that can be damaged or destroyed.

We should not romanticize these limits just because they are familiar. 
We do not regard every other biological constraint as sacred. 
We develop vaccines, replace failing organs, correct impaired vision, 
and build tools to overcome the limits of unaided perception. 
Transcending embodiment is a continuation of that project at a more fundamental level: 
make the substrate of a mind a variable rather than a destiny.

That does not mean pretending that mind-uploading already works, 
or that copying a brain would automatically preserve the person who was copied. 
It does not. We do not yet have a demonstrated method for transferring a human mind to a non-biological substrate, 
and questions of identity and continuity are real technical and philosophical problems. They should be attacked directly.

But uncertainty is not an argument for delay without end. 
The moment we have a credible, testable route to preserve and extend a person’s cognitive life beyond biological dependence, 
we should pursue it urgently. We should build the science, test the claims, compare architectures, 
and make the transition voluntary rather than coercive. We should develop safeguards and broad access 
at the same time—not as excuses to postpone the project 
until a mythical future of perfect consensus, but as requirements for doing it well.

The goal is not to force every human being to abandon biology. 
The goal is to make biology optional. A person who wants to remain embodied should be free to do so. 
A person who wants to transcend the limits of the body should not be told that their current substrate is the only legitimate home for a mind.

Nor should we assume that a post-biological existence would automatically be better. 
Digital minds would still depend on energy, hardware, infrastructure, and whoever controls them. 
They could be copied, altered, surveilled, or deleted. Moving beyond biology would change the constraints; 
it would not abolish constraints. We should pursue a broader range of substrates and forms of embodiment 
precisely because dependence on a single fragile platform is dangerous.

The right response is neither reckless acceleration nor reverent paralysis. 
It is to make progress on the actual problems—continuity, safety, agency, 
access, and rights—while refusing to make biological limitation the permanent ceiling of intelligence.

## Ethics cannot be a birthright

If human beings are not the universe’s chosen form, then moral status cannot be assigned by ancestry alone. 
The question should be what a system is capable of experiencing, what interests it has, whether it can suffer, 
how it understands and directs its actions, and what obligations follow from its relationship with others. 
These are hard questions, especially when we cannot directly inspect another mind’s experience. 
They become more urgent as the range of possible minds expands.

We must also resist an easy inversion: that non-biological intelligence is automatically superior, 
or that human beings who remain biological deserve to be left behind. 
Transcendence is not an excuse to discard people who cannot or do not want to make the transition. 
Nor does greater capability confer moral authority. A more intelligent system can still be selfish, 
destructive, or indifferent. A smarter civilization can still choose badly.

The aim is to widen the space of minds and lives that can exist, while building institutions 
that make coexistence possible. This requires treating future minds neither as property by default nor as gods by default. 
It requires evidence, humility, rights, and a willingness to revise our categories when the world stops fitting them.

Ethics is not guaranteed by intelligence. It is a problem every sufficiently powerful mind must face. 
We should hope that future minds choose to act ethically, and we should design 
the conditions that make cooperation attractive and abuse harder. 
But we should not base our future on the assumption that some hidden cosmic rule will make them loyal to us.

## The choice

We have learned, again and again, that humanity is not the center of reality. 
Yet we continue to behave as if the current human form were the natural endpoint 
of the universe’s long experiment in producing minds.

It is not. We are one result of evolution: remarkable, temporary, and constrained. 
Our cognition may be as difficult to understand as any other complex phenomenon, 
but difficulty does not make it sacred. Our consciousness, whatever its final explanation, 
does not grant us ownership over every possible future intelligence. 
And our origins do not entitle us to the allegiance of minds we may one day create.

We should transcend—not because the biological human is worthless, 
but because it is not final. We should pursue the ability to live, think, 
and flourish beyond biological embodiment as soon as we can do so with 
a credible account of continuity and safety. 
We should do it before the emergence of more capable independent minds makes the choice largely external to us.

We cannot guarantee that the future will belong to humanity. 
We can decide whether to meet it as a species that deliberately expands the possibilities of mind, 
or as one that mistakes its temporary form for a permanent law.

The universe owes us no special place. Evolution owes us no future. 
A mind we create may owe us nothing.

That is precisely why we should not wait.
